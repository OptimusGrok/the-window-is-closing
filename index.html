Part One · Fuel
Chapter 1

Encyclopaedia

A work of speculative fiction. Written in an imagined voice; not written, endorsed, or reviewed by Elon Musk. Events after 2026 are imagined.

I was born in Pretoria in June 1971. Winter there. Dry, cold mornings, warm afternoons, jacaranda trees that turn the whole city purple in spring. People who grew up there talk about the jacarandas. I mostly remember the books.

Let me be honest about something up front. This is not a reliable narrator situation. Memory is a lossy compression algorithm. It keeps the shape of a thing and throws away the details, then fills the details back in with whatever seems plausible. So when I tell you what I remember, I'm telling you what my brain rebuilt from fragments. Some of it is probably wrong. The broad strokes are right.

The broad strokes are: I read everything.

Not in a charming way. In a slightly alarming way. I would read for ten hours a day if nobody stopped me, and on weekends nobody stopped me. I read the books for children and then I read the books for adults and then I ran out of books at the school library and the local library and started on the encyclopaedia.

The Encyclopaedia Britannica is a strange object if you're eight. It was the closest thing to a full download of human knowledge that existed. Organised alphabetically. Terrible way to organise knowledge. Great way to read about aardvarks and Antarctica on the same afternoon. I just went through it. Volume by volume. I didn't really decide to do that. It was more like I picked one up and then didn't stop.

The effect was strange. I became the kid who knew things. Somebody at dinner would say, I wonder how far away the moon is, and I'd say about 384,000 kilometres, and then I'd explain the orbit was elliptical so it varied, and then I'd notice everyone was looking at me. I was not reading the room. I didn't know there was a room to read. I thought people asked questions because they wanted answers.

This turns out to be only partly true. Took me decades to figure that out. Still working on it.

✦

My mother, Maye, was a model and a dietitian. She was born in Canada, grew up partly in South Africa, and was the most practical person in any room she walked into. She would work several jobs at once and not complain about it. If you told her something was impossible, she would ask what specifically was impossible about it. I think I got that from her. Or I absorbed it. Same thing, at that age.

My brother Kimbal was born about fifteen months after me. My sister Tosca a couple of years after that. Kimbal and I fought constantly, the way brothers do, physically and verbally, and then we'd team up against anything outside the family. That pattern held for a long time. Still holds, sort of. Fewer punches now.

Tosca was the creative one. She'd stage little productions. Kimbal and I would get roped in as actors. I was a bad actor. Kimbal was better. Tosca would direct us both with total authority. She went on to make films, which surprised nobody who saw her at seven.

My father, Errol, was an engineer. He was smart. He could build things and he knew how buildings and machines worked. I'll say more about him later, but not much more. Some things belong to the family. Some things I've said publicly and don't need to say again. Our relationship was difficult. I'll leave it there for now.

✦

The books that mattered most, in roughly the order they hit me:

Asimov's Foundation series. The idea is that a mathematician, Hari Seldon, works out that the galactic empire is going to collapse and there will be thirty thousand years of dark age afterwards. He can't stop the collapse. But he can shorten the dark age to a thousand years by preserving knowledge in the right places and nudging events.

I read that and thought: obviously the correct move. If civilisation can fall, and history says it can, then you want to reduce the length and depth of the dark age. You want backups.

That thought never left. It just changed shape.

Then The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy. I was maybe twelve or thirteen and going through what I'd describe as an existential crisis, which sounds dramatic, but I think a lot of kids who read too much go through it. I'd read the philosophers and the religious texts trying to figure out the meaning of life. Nietzsche and Schopenhauer at fourteen is not a recipe for cheerfulness. I don't recommend it.

Then Douglas Adams comes along and basically says: the universe is absurd, the answer is 42, and the real problem is that you don't actually know what the question is.

That hit hard. Not as a joke. As a research agenda. If the hard part is figuring out the right question, then the goal should be to expand the scope and scale of consciousness so that we can understand the universe well enough to ask better questions. More minds. More knowledge. Bigger civilisation. Ideally across more than one planet, so one bad day doesn't end the whole project.

I know how that sounds coming from a twelve-year-old. It sounded weird then too. I didn't say it out loud much.

The Lord of the Rings. Every boy who reads goes through Tolkien. The heroes in those books don't want the job. They get handed something terrible and they carry it anyway, mostly because no one else will. I think I liked that more than I understood why.

Heinlein. The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress in particular. A revolution on the Moon, run partly by a computer that wakes up. Lunar colonists. Rail guns throwing rocks. The idea that the place you live doesn't have to be the place you were born.

And comics. Lots of comics. Superheroes are always trying to save the world. As a kid you don't notice how much that idea gets into you. You just think, well, yes, of course somebody should be trying to save the world. Why isn't everyone doing that?

✦

We built rockets.

Not real rockets. Model rockets, mostly, with the solid motors you could buy or, when you couldn't buy them, with chemistry we probably should not have been doing. Pretoria in the early eighties did not have a big hobbyist rocket supply chain. So you figure it out. You go to a pharmacy and buy the ingredients. You mix them in ratios you read in a book. You pack them into a tube. Sometimes it goes up. Sometimes it goes sideways. Once or twice it just goes.

Kimbal and I were lucky to keep all our fingers. I want to be clear that I'm not recommending this either. It is a miracle of statistics that we both still have ten.

What I learned: the gap between the drawing and the thing that flies is enormous. On paper, the rocket goes straight up. In the real world, the fins are slightly off, the motor burns unevenly, the wind gusts, the nose cone is heavier on one side because you used too much glue. Reality is full of small errors that compound.

That lesson took thirty years to fully sink in. Falcon 1 taught me the rest of it.

✦

I was also, and this is not a secret, a deeply weird kid.

I would go into what my family called a trance. Somebody would be talking to me and I wouldn't hear them. I'd be staring at nothing, working on something in my head. My parents thought I might be deaf. They took me to get my hearing tested. They even had my adenoids taken out, on the theory that it would fix it. It did not fix it. My hearing was fine. I was just elsewhere.

What was going on in there, as best I can describe it: the part of the brain that normally processes what you see out of your eyes was being used for internal visualisation. I could picture a machine and rotate it and see how the parts fit, and change the parts, and see what broke. It was like running a simulation. While that was happening, the outside world went quiet.

I still do it. Ask anyone who's been in a meeting with me. There's a pause, sometimes a long one, and people think I'm angry or bored or having a stroke. I'm just running the simulation. It's annoying for everyone else. Sorry.

✦

South Africa in the seventies and eighties was under apartheid. I was a white kid in Pretoria, which was the administrative capital, so I saw the system from the side of it that was protected. I didn't fully understand it as a small child. You absorb your world as normal until you're old enough to see it from outside. By my teens I could see it, and I didn't want to be part of it. There was also mandatory military service. I didn't want to serve in that military, enforcing that system. That became one of the reasons to leave. I'll come back to that.

But I want to be careful not to make myself sound more noble than I was. A big part of my reason for leaving was simpler. America was where the stuff got built. Silicon Valley. NASA. The companies on the covers of the magazines. If you wanted to work on the future, you went to the future's address. I was a kid with a list of things that ought to exist, and none of them were being built near me.

✦

Here's a thing that happened when I was maybe nine.

I was outside at night, which in the highveld means a very clear sky. Altitude around 1,300 metres, low humidity, not much light pollution back then. The Milky Way was a band across the whole sky. You could see it was a structure. Not a pattern of dots, a thing.

I remember doing a rough calculation. I'd read the numbers. A hundred billion stars, give or take, in our galaxy. Probably more. A lot of them with planets, though at that time nobody had confirmed even one planet around another star. That came later, in the nineties. But I thought: if even a tiny fraction have life, the sky should be crowded.

It wasn't crowded. It was quiet.

That's the Fermi paradox, though I didn't know the name for it then. If the universe is this big and this old, where is everybody? There are a lot of possible answers. Maybe life is rare. Maybe intelligence is rare. Maybe intelligent life tends to destroy itself before it spreads. Maybe it doesn't spread because it doesn't want to.

The answer I couldn't shake was the third one. That there's some filter, some point that civilisations reach and then don't get past. And that we don't know whether we've passed it yet.

If you take that seriously, even at nine, the conclusion is uncomfortable. Consciousness might be incredibly rare. We might be the only candle in a very large dark room. And the candle is sitting on one table. If the table tips over, that's it.

So you'd want more than one candle. On more than one table.

I didn't phrase it like that then. I'm phrasing it like that now. But the feeling was the same. I went inside and went to bed and didn't sleep for a while.

✦

Quick note on how I think about problems, because it started then and it'll come up a lot.

There's a way of reasoning that most of us use most of the time, which is reasoning by analogy. You look at how things have been done and you do something similar with small changes. This is efficient. It's how you get through a day. You don't derive the physics of walking every time you cross the room.

The other way is first principles. You break the problem down into the things you're sure are true, the fundamental physics or the basic facts, and you reason up from there. It takes much more mental energy. It's slow. You can't do it for everything. But when you do it on the right problem, you sometimes find that the accepted answer is wrong by a factor of ten.

As a kid, I didn't know that's what I was doing. I just didn't trust the explanations I was given unless I could see why they were true. If a teacher told me something, my instinct was to ask why, and then why again. This was not always well received. Some teachers loved it. Most found it exhausting. I understand that now.

✦

My grandparents on my mother's side are a story I loved. My grandfather, Joshua Haldeman, was a Canadian chiropractor who moved the family to South Africa in 1950. He and my grandmother flew their own single-engine plane across Africa, and once to Australia, back when that meant navigating with maps and a compass and occasionally landing on whatever was flat. They went searching for a lost city in the Kalahari. Repeatedly. They never found it. They kept going anyway.

I never met him. He died in a plane accident when I was very young. But the stories were family scripture. The lesson I took was not that you should fly a single-engine plane across the Indian Ocean, though that is extremely cool. It was that you could just go. That the world was bigger than the town you lived in, and the people who looked past the edge weren't crazy, or if they were, it was the useful kind.

My mother had that in her too. She'd moved countries. She'd later move again, with almost nothing, and rebuild everything. When I left at seventeen, she didn't treat it as madness. She treated it as logistics.

✦

If I had to compress my childhood into one sentence, it would be: a kid who read too much, built things that occasionally exploded, and couldn't stop thinking about whether humanity was going to make it.

None of which made me popular.

That part comes next.

The window is closing. I didn't have that phrase yet. But I had the feeling, even then, lying in bed after the Milky Way. That there was a limited amount of time to get things right. That technology doesn't automatically improve. That it can go backwards. The Egyptians built pyramids and then forgot how. The Romans built aqueducts and then for centuries nobody could. People think progress is a law of nature. It isn't. It's something people do, and when they stop doing it, it stops.

I didn't know what I'd do about that. I just knew I'd do something.

Chapter 2

Blastar

The first computer I ever saw that I could actually touch was in a shop in Johannesburg. I was about ten. It was a Commodore VIC-20. Five kilobytes of memory, of which about three and a half were usable. It plugged into a television. It had a keyboard that felt like a cheap calculator.

I stood in front of it like it was an altar.

The thing about a computer, which I understood instantly and couldn't have explained, is that it's a machine that becomes whatever you tell it to become. A hammer is a hammer. A car is a car. A computer is a hammer, a car, a game, a calculator, a library, a world. Depends on what you type.

I begged for one. I got one. It came with a workbook for learning BASIC that was supposed to take six months. I didn't sleep for about three days and finished it.

I want to say that was discipline. It wasn't. It was compulsion. I couldn't stop. Every concept led to another one. Variables. Loops. Conditionals. Each one opened up new possibilities, and each possibility needed testing, and the testing produced bugs, and the bugs needed fixing. It was the best feedback loop I'd ever been in. You do a thing, you see the result immediately, you adjust. No waiting. No adults. Just you and the logic.

✦

Three and a half kilobytes, for reference, is about the size of this paragraph and the two above it, stored as text. That was the whole universe you had to build in. Every byte mattered. You learned to be ruthless. If a variable name could be one letter instead of six, it was one letter. If a routine could be reused, it was reused. If a feature wasn't essential, it was gone.

That turned out to be one of the most important things I ever learned. Constraints aren't the enemy. Constraints force you to understand what actually matters. Engineers given infinite resources will build infinitely complicated things. Engineers given 3.5 kilobytes build something that works.

I still think about the VIC-20 when I look at an overdesigned part. Could this be done with less? Usually yes. Usually much less.

✦

When I was twelve I wrote a game called Blastar. It was a space shooter. You're a ship, there are alien freighters carrying hydrogen bombs and status beam machines, which is a phrase I'm not sure I'd defend now, and you blow them up. Simple graphics. It was in BASIC with some machine code. Maybe a few hundred lines.

I sent it to a South African computer magazine called PC and Office Technology. They published the code, which was how games got distributed back then. You'd type the listing in yourself from the page. They paid me about five hundred US dollars.

Five hundred dollars. At twelve. For something I made out of nothing but typing.

That was a revelation, and not mainly about money, though the money was great. It was the realisation that the barrier between "kid in his bedroom" and "thing that exists in the world and people pay for" was much thinner than anyone had told me. You didn't need permission. You didn't need to be an adult. You needed to make something that worked.

Somebody put Blastar online years later as a browser game. You can play it. It's not good. It's fine for a twelve-year-old. I'm fond of it in the way you're fond of a bad haircut in an old photo.

✦

Kimbal and I also tried to open a video arcade near our school. This was maybe a year or two later. We had worked out the economics: the machines were expensive, but the per-play income was high and there were no arcades in that part of town. We had a location. We had a supplier lined up, sort of. We had a business plan, more or less.

What we didn't have was an adult willing to sign the paperwork. You need a permit to run a business. Permits require someone over eighteen. Every adult we asked looked at us like we were insane.

I think about that sometimes. The idea was decent. The economics were fine. We were stopped by a rule about age. That's not a complaint. The rule exists for reasons. But it was my first encounter with the fact that most of what stops things from happening isn't physics. It's paperwork.

Paperwork is a much more formidable opponent than physics. Physics at least has consistent rules.

✦

Now the hard part.

My parents divorced when I was around eight or nine. My mother took us, at first. Then, a few years later, I decided to go live with my father. I made that decision myself. I was a kid and I thought he seemed lonely, and he had the Encyclopaedia Britannica and a computer, and I think I imagined I could help.

I've said publicly that it was a mistake. I'll stand by that and not elaborate much further. He was a brilliant engineer and he could be charming. He could also make life very hard for people around him. Kimbal came too, later. We both got something out of it in a strange way. You learn things under pressure. Mostly you learn things you'd rather not have had to learn.

People ask me about my childhood and they want a neat story. Either the trauma origin story where the dad makes the son into a machine. Or the happy story where everything was great and the kid was just gifted. Neither is right. It was complicated, which is what most childhoods are. I've decided that the useful thing is not to keep relitigating it. The useful thing is to try to be better as a father myself, which I'm not sure I'm managing either, honestly. It's a hard job. Harder than rockets.

That's all I'm going to say about my dad. Everything else is family.

✦

School was bad.

I was the youngest and smallest kid in my class for years. I'd skipped ahead partly. I was also not good at the social stuff. I'd correct people. I'd tell them facts they hadn't asked for. I'd walk around reading. To an adolescent pack, that's like wearing a sign that says please target me.

So they did.

It went on for years. Being chased. Being cornered. There was one time when a group of boys came at me on a set of concrete stairs at school. I was sitting at the top. One of them kicked me in the head from behind and then they pushed me down the stairs and beat me until I blacked out. I ended up in hospital. My nose needed surgery much later, as an adult, to fix the damage. I still have trouble breathing through it sometimes.

I'm not telling you this for sympathy. Lots of kids go through worse. I'm telling you because it explains some things.

It explains why I learned to fight. Eventually I learned judo and karate and wrestling, and I hit a growth spurt at around fifteen or sixteen, and things changed. Turns out a lot of bullying stops abruptly when the target can hit back and is now taller than the bullies. That's not a profound moral insight. It's just how primate dynamics work. We're very sophisticated monkeys. Emphasis on monkeys.

It explains why I retreated into books. Books didn't push you down stairs. Books were consistent. A good book does what it says it does, every time you open it.

And I think it explains a certain stubbornness. When you've been the target of a crowd, you get used to the crowd being wrong about you. Later in life the crowd was wrong about a lot of things. Electric cars couldn't work. Reusable rockets were a fantasy. Then, sometimes, the crowd was right about me, and I had to learn that too. Being used to being disliked can make you deaf to good criticism. That's a failure mode. I've fallen into it. I'll fall into it again probably.

✦

There's an opposite lesson from school that I learned, which took longer.

The kids who were nice to me mattered disproportionately. A few friends. A teacher or two who let me read during class without making a fuss. People who would sit next to you at lunch when sitting next to you had a social cost.

That's a kind of courage that doesn't get recognised much. It's not jumping into a fire. It's just choosing the weird kid over the comfortable option, a small amount of social risk, repeatedly. I've thought about that a lot when hiring. The people who will sit next to the weird idea at lunch. Those are the ones you want.

✦

Quick tangent about something I figured out on the VIC-20 that's still relevant.

When you program at that scale, you develop an intuition for what the machine is actually doing. Not the language. The machine. Every instruction you type becomes some number of operations on some registers in some memory addresses. You can almost feel it. The abstraction is thin.

Modern computing is the opposite. Abstraction stacked on abstraction stacked on abstraction. Most programmers have no idea what the hardware is doing underneath, and mostly they don't need to. That's a triumph of engineering. It's also dangerous, because when something goes wrong deep in the stack, very few people know where to look.

I think the same thing is true of civilisation. We use technology built on top of technology built on top of technology, and the number of people who understand the bottom layers keeps shrinking. How do you make steel? How do you refine silicon? How do you make a fertiliser plant work? If we lost the people who know, how fast could we get them back?

That's the Foundation problem again. Knowledge is fragile. Keep backups.

✦

A word about Kimbal and Tosca in those years, because they get left out of the story and shouldn't.

Kimbal was the one who could talk to people. I'd go into a shop and stand there doing maths on the prices. He'd go in and come out with a discount and the owner's life story. We were a decent team. I figured out what to build. He figured out who'd buy it. That division of labour lasted, more or less, into Zip2.

We also did a lot of dumb things together. We'd ride our bikes across Pretoria and into Johannesburg, which was a long way and not entirely safe, to go to computer shops and bookshops. Hours each way. We'd come back sunburned and broke with a new magazine or a floppy disk. Our parents were not always informed in advance.

Tosca was younger and mostly ignored us, correctly. She was building her own world. She read as much as I did, but fiction, mostly romance and drama, and she'd act out stories with total conviction. Years later she'd make films and run a streaming company for romance movies. I'm not surprised. She always understood what people wanted to feel. I understood what machines wanted to do. Different skill. Hers is rarer.

The three of us were close in the way kids are when the adults around them are complicated. You form a unit. You cover for each other. You fight constantly and never mean it.

Quick aside on what a ten-year-old learns from riding a bike sixty kilometres to buy a computer magazine: the information is worth the trip. If you want to know something badly enough, distance isn't the constraint. Willingness is. I've applied that to a lot of things since. Usually it's not a bike. Usually it's a phone call at three in the morning to an engineer who knows the answer.

✦

By fifteen I'd mostly worked out what I wanted. Not the companies, I couldn't have named those. But the categories. I wanted to work on things that would most affect the future of humanity. I didn't phrase it that grandly. I just knew that I wanted to work on important stuff, and the important stuff was not in Pretoria.

There was also the army. Every white South African male had to do compulsory service. I didn't want to. I didn't want to spend two years in a military whose main job, at that point, was enforcing apartheid. I didn't want to point a gun at people in townships in the name of a system I thought was wrong.

My mother was Canadian. That meant I could get Canadian citizenship. That meant I had a door.

I spent a while working on the paperwork. Remember what I said about paperwork. It took longer than I wanted. Everything takes longer than you want. That's basically the theme of my life, as anyone who has heard me give a timeline can confirm.

At seventeen, I had a Canadian passport, a plan that was mostly not a plan, and a small amount of money.

The window is closing. That's what I'd say to myself now. Back then, it felt more like: if I don't go now, I'll get stuck. I'll get drafted, I'll go to a local university, I'll get a job, I'll be an engineer in Pretoria, and in thirty years I'll look up at the Milky Way and wonder.

That seemed like the worst possible outcome. Worse than being poor. Worse than failing. The worst outcome was not trying.

So I went.

One-Way Ticket

I landed in Montreal in June 1989. I was seventeen. I had a backpack, a suitcase full of books, and a few thousand Canadian dollars, which sounds like more than it was.

My plan was to stay with a great-uncle. When I got to Montreal I found out he'd moved to Minnesota. I had written to him. The letter had arrived after he left, or never arrived, or arrived and nobody read it. Either way, nobody was waiting at the airport.

So I'm standing in Montreal with my bags and no place to go.

There's a moment like that in most lives. Maybe a few. The moment when the plan meets reality and the plan loses. The question is what you do next. You can panic, you can go home, or you can find a youth hostel.

I found a youth hostel.

✦

This wasn't heroic. It was just the next step. There's a lot of mythology around founders and their origin stories. I've contributed to some of it, I'm sure. The truth is usually more boring. You do the thing that obviously has to happen next. Then you do the next thing. If you string together enough next things without stopping, it looks like a plan from the outside.

The hostel was cheap. Dorm room, bunk beds, strangers snoring in five languages. I bought a bus ticket that let you travel across Canada for about a hundred dollars. And I had relatives, distant ones, mostly on my mother's side. I started working my way west.

✦

Montreal in summer, by the way, is beautiful. People are out. There are festivals. I didn't really notice. I was mostly figuring out how much food cost.

Here's the budget, roughly, because I remember being obsessed with it.

Hostel: a few dollars a night. Food: as little as possible.

I worked out I could live on about a dollar a day for food if I bought hot dogs and oranges in bulk, and pasta, and a jar of pasta sauce that would last a week if you didn't think too hard about it. I had actually tested this before, sort of. As a kid I'd wondered what the minimum cost of being alive was. Later, in Pennsylvania, I ran the experiment properly. Thirty dollars for a month. Pasta, hot dogs, oranges. Got a bit tired of oranges.

The point of the experiment wasn't to be miserable. It was to find the floor. If I knew I could survive on a dollar a day, then I could take almost any risk. The downside was bounded. I wasn't going to starve. I could always go back to hot dogs.

That's a strangely freeing thought. Most people's fear of failure isn't about failure. It's about not knowing how bad the bottom is. If you go look at the bottom, it's usually not as bad as you thought. Once you know that, the fear shrinks.

I've run that calculation, in one form or another, my whole life. What's the floor? Can I live with the floor? If yes, go.

✦

I ended up at a cousin's farm in Waldeck, Saskatchewan. Population, I think, a few hundred people. Wheat in every direction. The sky out there is enormous. It's a different enormous from the highveld. Flatter. You can see weather coming for an hour before it hits.

I worked on the farm. Shovelling grain. Fixing things. Learning which end of a cow does what. I was not a natural farmer. I was a kid who'd read the encyclopaedia volume on agriculture, which turns out to be very different from agriculture.

My eighteenth birthday was on that farm. There was a cake. I remember being grateful and slightly overwhelmed. These were people I barely knew, and they'd taken me in, and they made a cake. Strangers being kind for no reason. You remember that.

✦

From there I went to Vancouver. Took a few jobs. Cut logs with a chainsaw for a while, which was terrifying and loud. Then I got the job that paid best, because nobody wanted it.

I cleaned the boiler room at a lumber mill.

Let me describe this so you understand. You put on a hazmat suit. You crawl into a narrow tunnel that runs under or around the boiler, where there's no room to stand. In there, there's sludge and hot residue, steaming. You shovel it out into a wheelbarrow, or pass it to someone at the opening. Then you crawl back in and do it again. If anyone's not careful, you get cooked. Not dramatically, but enough.

Out of the original group who started, most quit within days. A few stayed. I stayed. It paid around eighteen dollars an hour, which was a lot then. Way more than farm work.

I'd love to tell you I learned some deep lesson about work and dignity in the boiler room. I mostly learned that it was hot. But there's something else, too. When I've been on factory floors since then, standing next to someone doing a hard, dirty, repetitive job, I don't see it as abstract. I know a bit about what it feels like. Not everything. A bit.

Later, when we were designing Optimus, I'd think about the boiler room. That's exactly the kind of job a robot should do. Dangerous, dirty, boring. No human being should have to crawl into that tunnel. It shouldn't be a test of how badly you need money.

✦

Those first months in Canada had a texture I've sort of lost access to. Being nobody. Being totally unknown. Nobody expects anything from you. Nobody's watching. You can't disappoint anyone because nobody's counting on you.

I don't want to romanticise it. It was lonely and I was broke. But it was also very clear. There was one variable: me. If something went wrong, I'd fix it or I wouldn't. If I needed money, I'd find work. There were no boards, no regulators, no shareholders, no press. There was a bus ticket and a list of relatives.

The contrast with later life is extreme. Later, every decision had a few thousand people downstream of it. Every sentence I said got analysed, often by people who'd decided in advance what it meant. Some of that I brought on myself. Some of it comes with the territory.

But the core of it, the core loop, was set in that year. You figure out what needs to happen. You figure out the floor. You go.

✦

My mother came over, eventually, with Tosca. Kimbal came too. Maye arrived in Canada and, I'll say it plainly, rebuilt her life from essentially zero, in a new country, in her forties, with not much money. She worked a bunch of jobs. She got back into modelling, which for a woman of that age at that time was unusual. She taught. She consulted as a nutritionist. She kept going.

People sometimes ask where I got my work ethic. I'm not sure there's a single answer. But if you'd watched my mother in those years, you'd have a strong candidate.

She was also very funny. Dry. She'd watch me agonising over something and say, well, are you going to do it or not. And I'd say, yes, probably. And she'd say, then why are we talking about it.

✦

There's an engineering point in here, and I'll make it briefly.

When you move countries with nothing, you do a kind of forced first-principles reasoning on your own life. All the defaults are gone. You have to figure out, from scratch: where do I sleep? What do I eat? How do I earn money? What's the actual minimum?

Most people never have to do that. Their defaults are inherited. They live in the city they grew up in, take the job that's available, rent the apartment their friends rent. The defaults are mostly fine. But they hide the real constraints. You never find out what's truly necessary and what's just habit.

Same with companies. Same with rockets. Most of what a rocket costs is habit. Most of the requirements on a part exist because somebody once wrote them down and nobody questioned them since. If you strip away the defaults and look at what physics actually requires, there's often a factor of ten hiding in there. Sometimes a hundred.

I didn't know I was learning that in a hostel in Montreal. But I think I was.

✦

The bus across Canada deserves a short entry, because it's the most Canada I've ever seen at once.

Montreal to Ontario, along the St. Lawrence. Then the long stretch north of the Great Lakes, through Ontario, which is mostly trees and lakes and rock for about a day and a half. It never ends. You fall asleep in forest and wake up in forest. You start to suspect the bus is going in a circle.

Then the prairies. Flat, wheat, sky. Manitoba, then Saskatchewan. Towns every fifty kilometres, each with a grain elevator, a gas station and a church. The bus would stop and people would get on and off and the driver knew half of them by name.

I read most of the way. I'd brought maybe a dozen books in my bag and I read them all twice. I'd also brought a notebook, and I'd write down ideas. Most of them were bad. Some were about computers. A few were about rockets, which at that point were pure fantasy. One page, I remember, was a list of energy sources ranked by how long they'd last. Oil, gas and coal were near the top for convenience and near the bottom for duration. Solar was at the bottom for convenience and at the top for duration.

I didn't know what to do with that list. I kept it anyway.

The first winter came that year too. Saskatchewan in winter can hit minus forty. At minus forty, Celsius and Fahrenheit are the same number, which is the only interesting thing about minus forty. Your nose hair freezes when you breathe in. Metal sticks to skin. Cars are plugged into electrical outlets overnight so the engine block doesn't freeze solid.

I'd grown up in a place where winter meant a jumper. I learned about heat transfer that year the hard way. Conduction, convection, radiation. Wind chill is convection. Wool works because it traps air. Cotton kills you when it's wet because water conducts heat away about twenty-five times faster than air. All of that was in the encyclopaedia. It's different when it's your ears.

✦

The window is closing. I had a different version then. It was: I'm eighteen, I'm in Canada, I can work, and I have maybe four years to get into a good university and get across the border to the United States before I'm old enough that it starts getting harder. Visas. Money. Momentum.

The plan had been America all along. Canada was the door. A good door. I ended up liking Canada a lot. But Canada was not where the rockets were.

So I applied to universities. Queen's, in Kingston, Ontario, said yes. Partly, I'll admit, I was swayed by the gender ratio. I was eighteen. My priorities were not entirely optimised for civilisation-scale outcomes.

In 1990 I went to Kingston with my books, my last few thousand dollars from the boiler room, and a vague but strong idea.

The idea was: figure out the most important problems. Then work on them.

That's not a business plan. But it's not nothing.

Three Things

Queen's University is in Kingston, Ontario, on the shore of Lake Ontario, where the lake drains into the St. Lawrence River. The winters are vicious. The wind comes off the lake at about thirty kilometres an hour and goes straight through whatever you're wearing. I'd grown up in Pretoria, where winter means a jacket in the morning.

I bought a better coat. Eventually.

I studied business and physics, more or less. Mostly I studied whatever I found interesting, which was a lot of things, and I skipped a fair number of lectures to read the textbook instead. That's not advice. Some lectures are excellent. But in general I found I could absorb information much faster by reading than by listening. A good reader can do maybe 400 to 600 words a minute. A lecturer talks at maybe 150. If the lecturer isn't adding something you can't get from the book, you're losing a factor of three or four on bandwidth.

I'll come back to bandwidth. It's an obsession.

✦

At Queen's I met Justine Wilson. She was a literature student, a writer, beautiful, sharp, and not at all interested in me at first. I asked her out. She said no, or rather she agreed to get ice cream and then didn't show up. I found her studying and got her a couple of ice cream cones anyway, which were melting by the time I got there.

We dated on and off over the next years. Later we'd marry. Later still things would fall apart. I'll get to that. But in Kingston she was the person I talked to about the things I didn't talk about with other people. Books. The future. What it would mean to do something that mattered.

I'll also say I learned a lot from her about how stories work. She wrote fiction. I tended to think in systems. She'd explain how a reader experiences a narrative, and why a plot that's logically perfect can still feel dead. I didn't fully understand. But it stuck around somewhere in my head. A product, it turns out, is a little bit like a story. People have to feel something, not just compute it.

✦

I made money at Queen's partly by building and selling computers. Buy the parts, assemble them, sell them to students. Margins were decent. I also ran a sort of informal nightclub-slash-party in a rented house with a friend. Charge at the door, pay for the rent. We'd turn the place into a big party for hundreds of people. It worked. It covered the rent for a month with one night.

That was a pattern I liked a lot. You find a thing people want, figure out how to provide it at a cost lower than what they'll pay, and keep the difference. That's all business is. Everything else is detail and execution and, mostly, not running out of money before the margins kick in.

✦

In 1992 I transferred to the University of Pennsylvania on a scholarship. That got me across the border, which was the whole point.

Penn had physics, which I loved, and Wharton, the business school, which I did because I figured I'd probably need to know how money worked if I wanted to build anything big. I ended up with two degrees, one in physics and one in economics. The physics was the real interest. The economics was insurance.

Physics, to me, is the most honest discipline. You can argue about almost anything else. You can argue about history and politics and literature forever. But if you drop a ball, it falls. If your rocket doesn't have enough delta-v, it doesn't get to orbit. Physics doesn't care about your opinion or your credentials or your social status. It doesn't care about consensus. Consensus has been wrong many times. Physics has never been wrong. Our understanding of it has been wrong. That's different.

The framework I took from physics, more than any specific result, was the way of thinking. Boil a thing down to the most fundamental truths you can find, then reason up. Check your answers against reality. Be willing to be wrong. Don't fall in love with your own theory.

That last one is hard. I've fallen in love with my own theories a few times. Reality always wins the breakup.

✦

At Penn, I spent a lot of time thinking about what I'd actually do with my life. I'd go on walks and ask myself, as a sort of thought experiment: what are the things that will most affect the future of humanity? Not in a business sense. In a civilisational sense. If you looked back from a hundred years in the future, what would have mattered?

I came up with a short list. Different versions, but it settled down to about three things, sometimes five.

The internet. In the early nineties, it was obvious, at least to some people, that the internet was going to be enormous. It was like humanity was developing a nervous system. Information that used to take days to move now took milliseconds. Every human being could, in principle, be connected to every other human being and to all knowledge. That's a phase change.

Sustainable energy. Civilisation runs on energy. We were getting almost all of it by digging up fossil fuels and burning them. That's a one-time inheritance. It runs out eventually, and in the meantime we're running a large uncontrolled experiment with the atmosphere. You can argue about how bad that experiment will be. But it's obviously not a good idea to run it at scale for longer than necessary. The sun is a giant free fusion reactor in the sky. It puts something like 170,000 terawatts on Earth. Humanity uses about 18 or 20. The gap is large.

Space, specifically making life multiplanetary. This is the candle problem from when I was nine. If we stay on one planet, eventually something kills us. Asteroid, supervolcano, war, a pandemic we can't stop, something we haven't thought of. The probability on any given year is small. The probability over a long enough time approaches one. A self-sustaining civilisation on Mars would be a backup. Life insurance for consciousness.

Then sometimes I'd add: artificial intelligence, which I thought would be the most powerful and most dangerous technology humanity would build. And rewriting human genetics, which I thought would be a big deal and also very fraught.

I didn't expect to work on all of them. I didn't expect to work on any of them, really. I just wanted to know what the list was. It's a lot easier to make good decisions about what to do when you know what matters.

The window is closing. For the first time I had a specific version of that. The window to get humanity off one planet might be open now and not forever. Civilisations go through peaks and troughs. We had the technology to reach the Moon in 1969 and then we stopped. Nobody's been back in over fifty years. That's not a straight line. That's a peak followed by a decline. If we don't use the window while it's open, it might close.

✦

Summer jobs. I got two that mattered.

The first was at Pinnacle Research Institute in Los Gatos, California. They were working on ultracapacitors, which store energy electrostatically instead of chemically. The appeal was that capacitors can charge and discharge enormously fast and survive many more cycles than batteries. The problem was energy density. Capacitors at the time stored far less energy per kilogram than batteries. I spent the summer reading about the physics and trying to think about how you'd make them dense enough to drive a car. I wrote a paper about it for school. I thought ultracapacitors might be the path to electric cars.

They weren't, as it turned out. Batteries improved faster. Lithium-ion won. But the exercise of thinking through energy storage at the physics level set me up for later. I knew what the numbers were and why they mattered.

The second job was at a video game company in Palo Alto called Rocket Science Games. Great name, which tells you something about my priorities. I worked on low-level code, drivers and such. Video games were where I'd started, with Blastar, and I loved them. Still do. But I'd started to feel that games, as fun as they are, weren't going to be on the list of things that changed the future. I wanted to be on the list.

Being in Silicon Valley that summer was electric. Everyone was working on something. There was money moving around. There were people in their twenties running companies. It was the opposite of Pretoria, where things were done the way they were done because they had been done that way. In Palo Alto, things were done the way they'd never been done because nobody had tried.

I knew that was where I needed to be.

✦

I'll add one more thing about Penn, because it shows how I was starting to think.

For a class, I wrote a paper on how solar power could get cheap enough to compete with fossil fuels. I argued that the price of solar cells would follow a learning curve, falling a fixed percentage for each doubling of cumulative production, and that eventually it would be the cheapest source of electricity. The professor gave it a good grade and, I suspect, didn't think it would happen anytime soon.

It took about thirty years. It happened.

The paper also argued that the big constraint wouldn't be the panels. It'd be storage. The sun goes down every night. Any solar future needs batteries, or some other way to store energy, at a scale that didn't exist. That's still roughly true. It's why Tesla Energy exists.

I'm not saying this to look prescient. I got plenty wrong in those papers. I underestimated how long it would take. I overestimated ultracapacitors. I had no idea about lithium-ion's trajectory. The point is that the shape of the problem was visible in the early nineties, to anyone who did the arithmetic. Most people didn't do it, because the conclusion was inconvenient for the businesses that existed.

That's a pattern. When the arithmetic says something big is coming and the existing industry says it isn't, check whose arithmetic it is.

✦

I applied to Stanford for a PhD in materials science and applied physics. The plan was to work on energy storage, ultracapacitors or batteries, which seemed like a direct line to sustainable energy. Stanford admitted me. I moved to California in 1995.

I lasted two days.

Here's what happened. The internet was going vertical. Netscape was about to IPO. Every week there was a new thing. I sat there thinking: I could spend five years in a lab making a slightly better capacitor, and probably the thesis wouldn't matter. Or I could go work on the internet right now, while it's being built, while the window is open.

I asked the department if I could defer. They said yes. I think everyone involved, including me, understood that deferral was a polite word.

I never went back.

I don't want to tell anyone that dropping out is good. For most people, finishing is a better choice. A PhD is valuable and research is important. But for me, in that moment, the opportunity cost of staying was enormous. The internet was being built once. It wasn't going to wait.

There's a decision rule I use a lot: if there's a big window and it might close, and the downside of jumping through it is survivable, jump. The upside of being early to something huge is so much bigger than the downside of being wrong. I had a floor. I knew I could live on a dollar a day. I knew how to build computers and write code. Worst case, I'd get a job.

So I jumped.

I had about two thousand dollars, a car that was falling apart, and student debt of over a hundred thousand dollars. I didn't have a business plan.

I had a brother who wanted in. That turned out to be enough.

Zip2

The idea was simple. Newspapers had business directories, yellow pages, city guides. Almost none of it was online. You couldn't look up a restaurant on the internet and get a map and directions to it. Not easily. The data existed in pieces. Nobody had put the pieces together.

So we'd put the pieces together. Business listings plus maps plus directions, on the web, sold to newspapers so they could have an online city guide. We called it Global Link Information Network at first, which is a terrible name. Then Zip2. Also not a great name, but shorter.

Kimbal was in. A friend named Greg Kouri, who we'd met in Toronto, put in money and became the third cofounder. Greg was older, steady, very practical. He was the adult in the room when the room didn't have one. He'd keep us from killing each other. He'd tell us when an idea was dumb, kindly. He died much too young, in 2012. I think about him a lot when I think about Zip2. He was a good man who bet on two kids with an idea, and was right to.

✦

We rented a small office in Palo Alto, on Sherman Avenue. The rent was cheap because it was a terrible office. No shower. Barely any furniture. Carpet of uncertain history.

I slept there. On a beanbag, mostly, under the desk sometimes. I'd code until I fell over, then sleep, then wake up and code. Kimbal and I had an apartment for a while but it was easier to just stay at the office. Showers happened at the YMCA down the road, for a few dollars.

People like to repeat this part as a kind of founder legend. Slept in the office, showered at the Y. It's true. But I want to be clear why. It wasn't to prove anything. It was because the commute was a waste of time and the apartment was a waste of money and the code wasn't going to write itself. Time and money were the two constraints. I was minimising both.

We had one computer at first. That's not an exaggeration. One machine running the web server. So during the day, the site would be up. At night, I'd take it down and use it to write code. Then put it back up in the morning.

That's the kind of thing you do when you have no money. It's also, I'd argue, a good way to learn exactly how your system works. When you only have one computer, you know every byte on it.

✦

Quick technical note, because people forget how primitive it all was.

In 1995 there was no Google Maps. There wasn't really any digital map you could put on a website. We licensed map data from a company that sold it for navigation systems, and a business directory database from another company. Then I wrote code to stitch them together so you could search for, say, pizza near an address, and get a list with a map and turn-by-turn directions.

The language was mostly C. The web was mostly static HTML. Making it interactive meant writing your own server-side code, and making it fast meant knowing how to squeeze performance out of hardware that would embarrass a modern toaster.

My code was functional and ugly. I learned to code by myself, in bedrooms and offices, and it showed. Later, when we hired real software engineers with real computer science degrees, they rewrote a lot of it. They were right to. There's a version of me that's slightly defensive about that. There's a better version that's glad they did.

✦

We raised money in 1996. A venture capital firm, Mohr Davidow, invested about three million dollars. That changed everything. We could hire people, rent a real office, buy more than one computer. It also came with a board and professional management.

The board brought in a CEO. An experienced executive. I became chief technology officer.

I didn't love that. I'm being honest. I was twenty-four or twenty-five. I wanted to run the company. I thought I knew what the product should be and where it should go, and I didn't like having that taken away.

But I also understand why the board did it. I was very young. I'd never managed anyone. My people skills were, let's say, a work in progress. I'd correct engineers in front of others. I'd fix their code at night without telling them. I thought I was helping. They thought, reasonably, that I was undermining them.

If you'd asked me then, I'd have said the board was wrong. If you ask me now, I'd say they were mostly right, and that I learned more from not being CEO than I would have from being it. Specifically, I learned what it feels like to lose control of something you built. That's a valuable feeling to have early, when the stakes are small. Later, it would happen again with bigger stakes.

✦

Kimbal and I fought. A lot. Physically, once or twice, in the office, which is not something I'm proud of. He's a very different person than me. More social, more intuitive about people. I was more of a systems guy. He'd want to do a deal one way, I'd want to do it another way, and neither of us knew how to back down.

There's one fight I remember where we were rolling around on the floor, and I cut my hand, and had to go get a tetanus shot. Afterwards neither of us could remember what the argument was about.

That's brothers.

I'd still trust Kimbal with my life. That's the thing about the fights. They happen inside the circle. Nobody outside gets to touch either of us. Later, when Tesla was nearly dying, Kimbal put money in that he couldn't afford to lose. That's the real measure.

✦

The strategy fight that mattered most at Zip2 was about whether to stay as a back-end provider for newspapers or go direct to consumers.

I wanted to go direct. Build the brand. Become the destination. Newspapers were our customers, but newspapers were also slow and they were going to get eaten by the internet. Building our future on their future seemed backwards.

The board and management wanted to stay with the newspapers. The newspapers were paying. Going direct to consumers meant competing with them, and maybe losing them, and the consumer business wasn't proven.

In hindsight I think I was right on the destination, and they were right on the timing and the risk. A consumer city guide could have been huge. But it would have needed a lot more money, and the probability that a small company would win against the giants that were about to show up was low. Being right about where the future goes doesn't mean you can get there before you run out of runway.

That's a lesson I have relearned, painfully, many times.

✦

There was a planned merger with a competitor, CitySearch, around 1998. On paper it made sense. Combine, become the clear leader, raise more money. I was against it. I thought the combined management was a mess and the culture would be bad. I pushed back hard enough that the deal died.

People thought I'd killed a good deal. Maybe I had. But I also think a bad merger is worse than no merger.

Shortly afterwards, in early 1999, Compaq offered to buy Zip2 for about $307 million in cash. Compaq wanted to bolster its AltaVista search engine. The board wanted to sell. It was a lot of money. I didn't have a strong argument against it.

My share was about $22 million.

✦

I want to describe what that felt like, because I think people imagine something cinematic.

It felt like a cheque arriving in the mail. I remember a magazine or TV crew filming me getting delivery of a McLaren F1 around that time, which I'd bought because it was, and is, one of the great engineering achievements in automobile history. Carbon fibre monocoque, gold foil in the engine bay for heat shielding, a centre driving position. I'd wanted one since I'd first read about it.

I also crashed it, a year or two later, trying to show off to a friend. Went airborne, spun, hit an embankment. No insurance. Totally my fault. Nobody hurt, thankfully.

So that's the lesson of sudden money: you will probably do something dumb with it. Hopefully something survivable.

Mostly, though, the money felt like ammunition. I'd been building something for four years that had become someone else's. Now I had the resources to build something that would be mine, where I could run it the way I thought it should be run.

And the internet was still going vertical. There was still a gap. The most obviously broken thing on the internet, in 1999, was money.

✦

On money as information. This is worth a short detour.

Most people think of money as a physical thing, coins, notes, gold. It isn't, mostly. It's a database. The money in your bank account is an entry in a computer. When you pay somebody, the bank subtracts from your entry and adds to theirs. That's it.

So what is money? It's an information system for labour allocation. It tracks who has done useful things for whom, and lets you trade that credit for other useful things. It's a ledger of obligations.

In 1999, that ledger was absurdly slow and expensive. A transfer between banks could take days. International transfers could take a week and cost a fortune. The systems were built on mainframes from the sixties, connected with batch processing that ran overnight. The banks made money from the friction.

If money is information, and the internet moves information at nearly the speed of light, then money should move at nearly the speed of light too. It didn't. That's a gap between what physics allows and what the system does. That kind of gap is where you build a company.

✦

The office in Palo Alto had one big advantage: it was cheap. It also had one big disadvantage: it was cheap.

The internet connection was a problem. We couldn't afford a proper one, so we drilled a hole in the floor and ran a cable down to an internet provider a floor below. That was our network. A hole. Very high-tech.

Kimbal and I had one computer at first. The website ran on it during the day. At night, I wrote code on it. So the site went down at night, more or less, which in 1995 nobody really noticed, because nobody was on the internet at night except us.

We showered at the YMCA. We ate at Jack in the Box, a lot. I still can't look at a Jack in the Box without feeling slightly ill and slightly nostalgic. Both at once.

The first salespeople we hired went door to door to businesses, trying to explain why they should pay to be listed on a website. Most of the businesses had never seen a website. One salesperson told me the most common response was: "What's the internet?" Then: "Why would I put my business on it?" Then: "Get out."

The arithmetic wasn't great. But it was getting better every month, because the number of people online was doubling roughly every year. When the denominator is growing that fast, today's terrible business is next year's okay business. The trick is to survive until then.

That's true of most new technologies. Early adopters pay too much for a worse product, and the company loses money on each one. Then volume arrives, costs drop, and the curve bends. You need to be alive when it bends.

✦

So I took most of the $22 million and put it into a new company. More than half of it, which was crazy, I was told. Probably right that it was crazy. I kept enough to live on. I didn't want a house. I didn't really want things, except the McLaren, and I'd already bought that.

The question I asked myself was: what's the floor? If X.com fails, I lose the money I put in. I keep some. I can still build computers and write code. I've lived on a dollar a day. The floor is fine.

The window is closing. The internet economy was going to be built in the next few years, and the companies that defined how money worked online would be defined then. If I waited, someone else would do it, and they'd do it worse, or at least differently.

So I didn't wait. I never really have.

That's a trait, not a virtue. Sometimes it's the right trait. Sometimes you should wait. Knowing which is the trick, and I've not always got it right. With X.com, I'd get it mostly right, and lose control again anyway.

It would almost kill me. Literally, as it turned out. Though not in the way you'd guess.

X

X.com launched in 1999 with an ambitious goal: be a full online bank. Checking, savings, brokerage, insurance. All of it. On the internet. Instantly.

It was a stupid name in some ways. People thought it sounded like a porn site. But I liked it. A single letter, simple, open-ended. X is the unknown in an equation. X marks the spot. I've stuck with that letter for a long time. Possibly too long. My kids would agree.

We did a lot of things in the first year, many of them badly. Banking regulations are a nightmare. Every state has its own rules. Every product needs its own approvals. Getting a banking license, or partnering with a bank that has one, took months. We had a tiny team trying to build a bank, which is like trying to build a cathedral with a few guys and a ladder.

But one feature took off. It was almost a side thing: you could send money to someone by email address. You'd type their email, an amount, and it would go. If they didn't have an account, they'd get an email telling them to sign up to collect.

That was viral. Every payment was an advertisement. Every recipient was a new user. We gave people twenty dollars for signing up and ten for each referral, which was expensive, but the growth was exponential. We went from zero to hundreds of thousands of users in a few months.

✦

There was another company a few blocks away doing roughly the same thing. Confinity, with a product called PayPal. Peter Thiel and Max Levchin, and a group of very sharp, very intense people.

We competed hard for a while. Both companies were burning money on signup bonuses. Both were spending a fortune fighting fraud, which was a massive problem in online payments then. Max, in particular, was a genius on fraud. Later he'd build systems that were among the best in the world for detecting it.

In early 2000 we merged. It made sense. Two companies spending everything to beat each other, when together we could dominate. The merged company kept the X.com name at first. I was the largest shareholder and became CEO.

The merger was rough culturally. The Confinity people were mostly Linux guys. My X.com engineers were more Microsoft. There was a huge argument about whether to rebuild everything on Windows or Unix. I wanted Microsoft because I thought the tools were better for moving fast. Most of the Confinity engineers thought that was insane.

I've since come around. They were probably right on the technical merits. But at the time it became a proxy for every other cultural difference, and I pushed it hard, and that was a mistake.

✦

I also wanted to drop the PayPal brand and call everything X. That was another mistake, I think, in hindsight, at least for the time. People liked PayPal. It was friendly. It explained what it did. X was abstract. You have to know when the brand people already love is more valuable than the brand you love.

So there were tensions. Then, in September 2000, I left for a trip. Partly honeymoon, since Justine and I had married that January and hadn't had time to take one. Partly to raise money. I was on a plane to Sydney for the Olympics, with planned meetings along the way.

While I was in the air, the board met and replaced me as CEO. Peter Thiel became CEO.

I found out when I landed.

✦

I want to describe how I handled it, because it's one of the few times I think I handled something like that well.

My first reaction was anger. I flew back. I tried to persuade the board to reverse the decision. They didn't.

Then I made a choice. I could fight, which would probably destroy the company, or I could accept it and support the company as a shareholder and advisor. Those were the options. Fighting might feel better for a while. But I believed in the company, and the company needed to survive more than I needed to run it.

So I accepted it. I stayed on as an advisor. I kept investing. I kept my stake.

People who know me now might find that surprising. I'm not famous for letting go. But I think the lesson from Zip2 had sunk in. Losing control isn't the worst outcome. Losing the company is the worst outcome. Sometimes the company is better off without you running it, at least for a while. That hurts. But it's true.

Peter and I later became, I'd say, friendly. We had real disagreements. But I respected him. He's one of the most original thinkers I've met. He was right about a lot of things. He was also very calm under pressure, which was useful when I wasn't.

✦

The malaria happened around the end of that year. I'd taken a trip to South Africa and Brazil. I was careless about precautions, as I often am about my own health. A few weeks after I got back to California, I collapsed.

It was falciparum malaria. The deadly kind. It reaches the brain.

The first doctors misdiagnosed it as viral meningitis, I think. The treatment was wrong. I got worse. A doctor who happened to have seen malaria cases recognised it and switched me to the right drugs, very aggressive ones. I was told afterwards that I was close to the point where it wouldn't have mattered. Maybe a day or two. Maybe less.

I spent around ten days in intensive care. Lost something like twenty kilograms. It took months to recover.

A short explainer on fraud, since it nearly killed PayPal and it taught me something I've used everywhere since.

In 2000, online payment fraud was growing faster than our revenue. Criminals, many of them organised, would open accounts with stolen credit card numbers, move money through a chain of accounts, and cash out before anyone noticed. At one point we were losing millions of dollars a month. If the losses had kept growing at that rate, the company would have been dead within a year.

The naive approach is rules. If a transaction is over some amount, flag it. If an account is new, limit it. Rules work for a while. Then the attackers learn the rules and route around them. Every rule you write is a puzzle they solve.

What Max and his team did was different. They built systems that looked at patterns across huge numbers of transactions, figured out what fraud looked like statistically, and adapted as the fraudsters adapted. Plus a clever trick: making suspicious users prove they were human by reading distorted text, one of the early versions of what became CAPTCHAs.

The lesson I took: in any adversarial system, static defences lose. You need a system that learns at least as fast as the adversary. That applies to fraud, to cybersecurity, to spam on a social network, and, later, to AI safety.

It also taught me to respect the attacker. The fraudsters were smart. Very smart. If you assume the other side is stupid, you've already lost.

Two percent.

Quick physics, because it matters for everything that comes next.

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky worked out the rocket equation over a century ago. It says: delta-v, the change in velocity you can get, equals exhaust velocity times the natural log of starting mass over final mass.

In plain terms: to go faster you either push the exhaust out faster, or you carry more fuel relative to the rest of the rocket. But it's a logarithm. Doubling your fuel doesn't double your speed. It adds a fixed amount. To get to orbit you need about 9.4 kilometres per second when you include gravity and drag losses. With chemical rockets, which is all we've got for launch, that means the rocket is mostly propellant. Something like 90 to 95 percent of the mass at liftoff is fuel and oxidiser. The structure, engines, and payload are the remaining few percent.

So a rocket is basically a very thin, very lightweight can of explosive liquid with an engine at the bottom. Every kilogram of structure you save is a kilogram of payload you gain. That's why aerospace is obsessive about mass.

The consequence: rockets are hard, but they're not made of exotic magic. They're mostly propellant and aluminium. The propellant is cheap. Liquid oxygen costs less than milk, per kilogram. Kerosene or methane is a commodity.

So the cost of a rocket isn't the stuff. It's how the stuff gets put together. And, crucially, that we throw it away after one use.

Imagine if airplanes were thrown away after every flight. A 747 costs a few hundred million dollars. If you had to throw it away after flying from Los Angeles to London, each ticket would cost a million dollars. Nobody would fly.

That's where rockets were. Single use. Every flight a brand new airplane, thrown into the ocean.

I didn't think we could fix reusability right away. That would take years, and money we didn't have. But I thought we could fix the idiot index. Build it ourselves. Vertically integrate. Make most parts in-house. Simplify the design. Iterate fast.

Then, later, reusability. If we survived that long.

A note on the Merlin engine, since it's the heart of everything.

An engine like Merlin works roughly like this. You have two propellants, kerosene and liquid oxygen. You need to pump them into a combustion chamber at very high pressure. The higher the chamber pressure, the more efficient the engine, roughly speaking, and the smaller and lighter it can be for a given thrust.

To get that pressure, you need a turbopump. A turbine spins a pump at tens of thousands of RPM, pushing propellant into the chamber. What spins the turbine? On Merlin, a small gas generator burns a bit of the propellant, rich in fuel, and that hot gas drives the turbine, and then it's dumped overboard. That's the gas generator cycle. It's simpler than fancier cycles, and slightly less efficient, since you're throwing some propellant away.

The injector, which mixes the propellants as they enter the chamber, was a pintle design. That's basically a central post that sprays fuel and oxidiser in a sheet. Tom knew pintles from TRW. They're very stable, which matters because combustion instability, where the flame starts oscillating violently, can destroy an engine in milliseconds.

The early Merlins used an ablative chamber. The inner wall is made of a material that slowly chars and erodes as it burns, carrying the heat away. That's simple and robust for a single-use engine. Later we went to a regeneratively cooled chamber, where the fuel runs through channels in the wall to keep it cool before it's burned. That's much better for reuse.

That switch, from ablative to regeneratively cooled, would later cost us a rocket. I'll explain when we get there.

Here's something I'd do with engineers that people found strange. I'd ask them to explain how their part could be removed.

Not improved. Removed.

The best part is the one that isn't there. It doesn't weigh anything. It doesn't cost anything. It can't fail. Every part you add is a part that can break, that has to be made, that has to be inspected, that has to be tested.

So the first question with any design was: can we delete this? If not, why not? Show me the requirement. Who wrote it? Why?

Often the answer was, it was in the last design, or the standard says so, or I thought it'd be safer. That's not good enough. The requirement has to have a name on it. A person. And that person has to be able to explain it.

Requirements from smart people are the most dangerous ones, because nobody questions them. I said that often. I still say it. Everyone's wrong some of the time. The question is whether you find out before or after launch.

I made a rough rule that if you're not adding back at least ten percent of the things you deleted, you didn't delete enough. Being too aggressive is fixable. Not being aggressive enough leaves you with a rocket that's too heavy and too expensive and has too many parts that can fail.

Why start with an expensive sports car?

This was actually the whole strategy, and in 2006 I wrote it down on the company blog in a short post, sort of tongue in cheek, as the "secret master plan." It went roughly like this:

One. Build a sports car. Low volume, high price.

Two. Use that money to build an affordable car. Medium volume, medium price.

Three. Use that money to build an even more affordable car. High volume.

And while doing all that, also provide zero-emission electric power generation options.

The logic was about cost curves. Any new technology starts expensive. Cell phones started as bricks for rich people. Computers started as rooms. The first units are always costly because you haven't optimised the design, you don't have economies of scale, and you're paying for development. So you sell to people who'll pay a lot and use that to fund the next, cheaper version.

The mistake would be to start with a cheap car. You'd lose money on every one and go bankrupt before you got to scale. The big automakers had sort of tried that. GM built the EV1 in the nineties, leased a thousand or so, then took them back and crushed most of them.

There was a lot of skepticism. Fair. No car startup had succeeded in America in a very long time. The last one to really make it was, arguably, Chrysler, in the 1920s. Building cars is incredibly hard. Not designing them, building them. The prototype is easy. The factory is the hard part.

I'd learn that the hard way.

✦

A quick note on energy density, since everyone asked why electric cars hadn't worked before and the answer is mostly here.

Gasoline is amazing stuff. It stores about 46 megajoules per kilogram. That's around 12 to 13 kilowatt-hours per kilogram of chemical energy. A lithium-ion battery cell in 2004 stored maybe 0.15 to 0.2 kilowatt-hours per kilogram. Call it sixty to eighty times less.

That sounds hopeless. It's not, for two reasons.

First, a gasoline engine wastes most of its energy as heat. A good car engine converts maybe 25 to 30 percent of the fuel's energy into motion at the wheels on average, often less in city driving. An electric motor and inverter convert something like 85 to 90 percent of the battery's energy into motion. So the usable gap is much smaller. Maybe twenty times, not sixty.

Second, you don't need as much stored energy as the gas tank holds. A fifty-litre tank holds about 450 kilowatt-hours of chemical energy, of which you'd use around 120 at the wheels. A decent electric car needs maybe 50 to 80 kilowatt-hours to go 250 miles or so. At 2004 cell densities, that's around 400 kilograms of cells, plus packaging. Heavy, but not insane. A sports car can carry it.

And lithium-ion was improving. Every year the energy density went up a few percent and the cost per kilowatt-hour went down a lot. That's the key insight. You don't bet on where the technology is. You bet on where the curve is going.

If you extrapolated the curve, batteries would eventually make electric cars cheaper than gasoline cars to build, not just to run. That was the end game. It took about twenty years.

✦

The Roadster was built on a Lotus Elise chassis. The idea was to take the Lotus, swap in the electric drivetrain, and change a few body panels. Simple.

It was not simple.

By the end we'd changed something like ninety-three percent of the parts. The body was carbon fibre. The chassis was modified to fit the battery. The battery pack was 6,831 laptop cells, wired together, with a cooling system and a lot of electronics to make sure if one cell overheated it didn't take the rest with it. The transmission was a nightmare. The original design had two speeds and kept breaking. We eventually went to a single-speed.

Every one of these things cost time and money. The cost to build each Roadster went way over what we'd planned. At one point, the cost per car was higher than what we were selling it for. That's not a business. That's a donation.

I was spending more and more time on Tesla. Much more than a chairman normally would. I was very involved in the design. I wanted the car to be great, not just good. I pushed for things that added cost, like the carbon fibre body and better seats and doors that were easier to get into. Some people thought this was me meddling. Some of it was. I think the car was better for it.

But the company had management problems. Costs were out of control and the schedule kept slipping. In 2007, the board decided Martin should step back from the CEO role. That was hard. A couple of interim CEOs came and went. The company was burning money and the Roadster still wasn't shipping.

The first Roadsters were delivered in early 2008. They were great cars. They were also being built at a loss, slowly, with a supply chain stretched across the planet. Battery packs assembled in California, bodies made in Europe, cars assembled partly in England.

It was a miracle any of them got built.

✦

The other company I'd put money into around then was SolarCity.

My cousins, Lyndon and Peter Rive, had grown up in South Africa too. Very smart, very entrepreneurial. In 2004 Lyndon and I were driving to Burning Man in an RV, and he was asking what he should do next. I said solar. The panels were getting cheap enough, but installing them was expensive and confusing and required people to pay a lot up front. Somebody should fix the installation and financing. Make it simple. Make it a lease, so people pay nothing up front and save money on their bill from day one.

Two years later, Lyndon and Peter started SolarCity. I was chairman and the largest investor. They did the work. They built it into the biggest residential solar installer in the US. Later, Tesla bought it, which was controversial. I'll get to that.

Solar, by the way, was the part of the plan people understood least. They thought Tesla was about cars. It was about energy. The car is a way to use electricity instead of oil. But where does the electricity come from? If it's from coal, you've moved the problem. If it's from the sun, you've solved it.

The sun puts enough energy on Earth in about an hour and a half to power human civilisation for a year. The amount of land you'd need to cover with solar panels to power the entire United States is something like a hundred miles by a hundred miles. That sounds like a lot until you realise how much land the US has, and that most of the panels could go on roofs.

Then you need batteries, for night. Then you've got a sustainable energy economy. Solar, batteries, electric cars. That's the full picture. It's not complicated. It's just big.

✦

So by 2007 I was chairman of Tesla, which was burning money building a car it was losing money on. CEO and CTO of SpaceX, which had launched once and blown up and was preparing to try again. Chairman of SolarCity, which was growing fast and needed capital.

And married, with five small boys.

I was also starting to understand something unpleasant about my plan. I'd assumed that if things went badly, they'd go badly one at a time. That one company could fail without dragging the others down. That I'd have time between crises.

That assumption was wrong. Crises correlate. When the economy turns, it turns for everyone at once. When money gets tight, it gets tight everywhere. When you're running out of cash in one company, you can't count on the others to save you, because they might be running out too.

The window is closing. In late 2007, if you'd asked me what the odds were that all three companies would be alive in two years, I'd have said less than fifty percent. If you'd asked me the odds that all three would be alive and I'd still have any money, I'd have said less than that.

I did not know, then, that 2008 was coming.

Most people didn't.

Chapter 10

Omelek

Kwajalein Atoll is a ring of about a hundred small islands around one of the largest lagoons in the world. It's roughly halfway between Hawaii and Australia. The US Army runs a missile test range there. The biggest island, Kwajalein itself, has an airstrip, a small town, a golf course and a lot of bicycles. Omelek, where we launched, is about thirty kilometres north across the lagoon. It's about the size of a few football fields. Palm trees, coral, sand, and the occasional crab.

To get there you'd fly to Honolulu, then catch the island-hopper flight, which stopped on some atolls along the way. Then a boat across the lagoon to Omelek. The trip from El Segundo took the better part of two days if everything went well. Nothing ever went well.

We built a launch site on Omelek from scratch. A concrete pad. A hangar, which was basically a tent at first and later a slightly better tent. A fuel farm. Our team lived on Kwajalein island in Army housing and commuted by boat every morning. When the weather was bad, which was often, the boats couldn't run.

The engineers there were tough. They'd spend weeks, sometimes months, on an island in the Pacific, doing the hardest job they'd ever done, with bad internet and no family around. They improvised constantly. If a part broke, the replacement was a week away. So they fixed the part. Or they made a new one with what they had. The place taught resourcefulness by force.

I went out several times. I don't think I was very helpful. Mostly I asked questions.

✦

The first launch was March 24, 2006.

I was in the control room, which for that flight was on Kwajalein island, watching on screens. The countdown went. The Merlin lit. The rocket lifted off and climbed. For about twenty-five seconds, I was the happiest I'd been in years.

Then there was a fire on the engine. You could see it on the camera. Flame where flame shouldn't be. The engine lost pressure, shut down, and the rocket fell back toward the island. It hit the reef close to the launch site. The payload, a small Air Force Academy satellite, came down on the roof of our machine shop.

It took us a few days to collect the pieces. Our team went out snorkelling on the reef to find fragments. Then they went through every piece of debris, laid it out on the hangar floor, and worked out what had happened.

What had happened was a nut.

An aluminium B-nut on a fuel line had corroded. Kwajalein is in the middle of the ocean. The air is warm, wet and full of salt. We'd been sitting on the pad for a long time, months, waiting for range availability and fixing other problems. The salt had worked into that nut and weakened it. It cracked. Kerosene leaked onto the hot engine. Fire.

A nut. A nut that cost a few dollars. In a rocket that had cost us years.

We switched to stainless steel for those fittings. We changed a lot of other things. We learned to protect the vehicle from salt air. A lot of the lessons from that flight are just basic, boring, unsexy engineering. Corrosion, fasteners, procedures. That's what kills rockets. Not the exotic stuff. The basic stuff.

✦

The press was harsh. Fair enough. Internet billionaire's rocket blows up. People had been waiting for that headline.

I wrote a short update afterwards, saying that we'd figure it out and fly again, and that SpaceX was in it for the long haul. I meant it. Privately, I was worried. We'd spent about four years and a lot of money to get to twenty-five seconds.

The team was devastated. I tried to keep them going. The way I did that, mostly, was to focus on the next flight. This is what happened. Here's why. Here's what we're changing. Here's when we try again. When something blows up, you don't want speeches. You want a plan.

✦

The second flight was March 21, 2007.

It went much better. The first stage burned through. Stage separation worked. The second stage lit and pushed the vehicle into space. We were above the Kármán line, above a hundred kilometres. We were in space.

People in the control room were cheering. Then the second stage started to wobble.

It was a slow, growing oscillation. The video from the onboard camera showed the Earth below rotating. Rocking side to side, then more. Then the stage started rolling, and then the engine shut down early.

What had happened was propellant slosh. As the second stage burned, the liquid oxygen in the tank started sloshing back and forth. The control system tried to correct for the motion it measured, but the slosh was coupling with the steering, and each correction made the oscillation worse. Eventually the engine sucked in gas instead of liquid and cut off.

We didn't make orbit. We'd got very close. Something like ninety percent of the way.

I called it a success, publicly. That was partly spin, I'll admit. But it was also true. We'd proven the first stage worked. We'd proven separation. We'd proven the second stage could light in space. The problem was a control issue, a solvable one. We added slosh baffles to the tank and changed the control software.

Close isn't orbit, though. Our customers needed orbit. My bank account needed orbit.

✦

Between the second and third flights, we made a big change. We upgraded the Merlin engine to a new version, Merlin 1C, with a regeneratively cooled chamber instead of the ablative one.

I mentioned this before. In a regenerative chamber, fuel flows through channels in the chamber wall before it's burned. That keeps the wall cool. It's how most high-performance engines work. It's more efficient, more reliable, and it's reusable, because the wall doesn't burn away.

We needed it for Falcon 9. Falcon 9 would have nine Merlins on its first stage. We wanted them to be the same engine as Falcon 1, so we'd be flight-proven. So we flew the new engine on Falcon 1, flight three.

That was the right decision. It also cost us the flight.

✦

Flight three, August 2, 2008, local time.

This one had real payloads. An Air Force satellite called Trailblazer. Two small NASA satellites. And, this is true, the cremated remains of a few people whose families had arranged a memorial spaceflight, including the actor who played Scotty on Star Trek and one of the original Mercury astronauts, Gordon Cooper. We wanted very much for them to get to space.

The launch looked perfect. The first stage burned, the Merlin 1C working beautifully. The first stage cut off. Separation.

Then the camera on the second stage showed the first stage bump into it.

The new engine had a characteristic we didn't fully account for. After shutdown, a regeneratively cooled engine has residual fuel in the cooling channels and in the lines, and that fuel keeps vaporising and producing a little thrust for a second or so. Not much. Enough. With the old ablative engine, the thrust decayed faster. With the new one, it lingered.

So when the stages separated, the first stage was still pushing slightly. It drifted forward and bumped the second stage just as it was trying to light. That collision damaged the second stage, or at least knocked it off course. We didn't make orbit.

The fix was simple. Add a few seconds of extra time between first-stage shutdown and separation, so the residual thrust dies out completely. That's it. A timing change. A couple of seconds.

But we'd lost the flight.

✦

That night was bad.

We'd launched three times. Three failures. Corroded nut. Slosh. Residual thrust. Each one different, each one something we should have caught, each one small.

I went to the team in El Segundo and Kwajalein, through video, and said, essentially: we're going to fly again. We have parts for a fourth rocket. We'll fix the timing problem and fly in a matter of weeks.

I said that with more confidence than I felt. The truth was that the money was nearly gone. I'd put in about a hundred million dollars at this point. SpaceX had other revenue from future launch contracts, but those customers would get nervous after three failures. I had enough left to fund maybe one more flight.

If the fourth one failed, that was it. SpaceX would be over.

And the fourth flight was on the same rocket design that had just failed three times.

✦

Let me do some honest maths here.

If each flight had a fifty percent chance of success, three failures in a row would happen about one time in eight. That's unlucky but not crazy. If each flight had a thirty percent chance, three failures would happen one time in three. More likely than not that we just weren't ready.

The key question was: are the failures random, or are they telling you something fundamental is wrong?

I spent a long time on that. Each failure was a different thing. None of them were fundamental design flaws. The nut was corrosion management. The slosh was control tuning. The recontact was a timing parameter. Each one had a clear cause and a clear fix. That's actually good news, in a strange way. It means you're working through the list of failure modes, one at a time. Each flight retires the problem it found.

The bad news is that you don't know how long the list is.

I thought we had maybe a decent chance. Fifty, sixty percent, on the fourth one. Not good enough to feel calm. Good enough to try.

✦

The team were relentless in those weeks after flight three. They had already been working insane hours for years. Now they worked harder. They had the fourth rocket, which had been built as a spare, and they had to get it out to Kwajalein and ready to fly in about seven weeks.

The first stage went out by ship, normally. But that would take too long. So they arranged to fly it on a C-17, a big military cargo plane, from Los Angeles to Kwajalein, via Hawaii.

The C-17 flight nearly ended in disaster. As the plane descended into Hawaii, the change in air pressure started crushing the rocket's tank. The tank was empty and sealed and it was a very thin-walled structure, built to be pressurised, and when the pressure outside went up faster than inside, it began to buckle. You could hear it, apparently, the metal groaning.

The engineers on the plane ran around opening valves to let air in. The pilot slowed the descent. It was a close call. When they got it to Kwajalein, the stage was dented. They popped the dents back out, inspected it, replaced some things, and decided it was fine.

That's what that team was like. They'd fix a crushed rocket on an island and launch it.

✦

What does it actually feel like to wait for a rocket launch you can't see?

The control room for the Kwajalein launches was a mix. Some of the team was on Omelek, a tiny island maybe the size of a few football fields. Some was on Kwaj, the main island, about thirty kilometres away. Some, including me for some of the launches, was in California watching data come in over a satellite link.

You stare at numbers. Chamber pressure. Tank pressures. Turbopump speed. Guidance state. Each one has a range it should be in. If it's in the range, it's green. If not, it's red. You're watching for red.

The countdown has holds and recycles. Something reads slightly off, and the launch director stops the count. You fix it, or decide it's a sensor issue, and restart. That can happen five, six times. Each time your stomach drops and then rises again. It's exhausting in a way that has nothing to do with physical effort.

Then ignition. Then the first few seconds, where the rocket is close to the ground and anything can happen. Then you start to breathe. Then max-Q, maximum aerodynamic pressure, where the rocket's under the most stress. Then stage separation. Then the second stage lights. Or doesn't.

On the island, people slept in tents and old buildings. They fixed things with whatever they had. The nearest real supplier was thousands of kilometres away. If a part broke, you either fixed it or waited weeks. That built a certain kind of engineer. Practical. Unfussy. I'd want those people on Mars.

✦

The window is closing. That was the summer of 2008. Lehman Brothers was about to fall. Tesla was running out of money. My marriage was ending. SolarCity was trying to raise capital in a market that was about to freeze.

And I had one more rocket.

I didn't sleep much. When I did, I had nightmares. Not about rockets. About disappointing people. The engineers who had given years to SpaceX. The customers who'd bet on us. The kids.

I'd be lying if I said I wasn't scared. I was scared all the time. I just didn't think fear was a good enough reason to stop.

Christmas Eve

Flight four lifted off from Omelek on September 28, 2008.

I didn't go to Kwajalein for it. I watched from the control room in Hawthorne, where we'd just moved the headquarters, in a big building that used to make fuselage sections for Boeing 747s. Kimbal was there. A lot of the team were there. Hundreds of people, standing in the factory, watching a screen.

There was no real payload. After three failures, nobody wanted to put a satellite on our rocket. So we flew a mass simulator, a dummy of about 165 kilograms. We called it RatSat. That felt about right for our standing at the time.

Countdown. Ignition. Liftoff.

The first stage burned. Every second of that burn felt about ten seconds long. Then first-stage shutdown. Then the extra delay we'd added, the few seconds for residual thrust to die away. I was counting them in my head. Then separation.

Clean.

Then the second stage, the little Kestrel engine, lit. The camera showed the first stage tumbling away, well clear, glowing in the sunlight. And then the second stage burned, and burned, and the Earth curved below it, and the oscillation we'd seen on flight two didn't come back, and the engine kept burning.

Then it cut off. On schedule. In orbit.

The factory exploded. People screaming, hugging, crying. Someone was jumping on a table. I think I was shouting. Kimbal grabbed me. I remember feeling physically lighter, like gravity had changed.

First privately developed liquid-fuelled rocket to reach Earth orbit. Six years after we'd started, in a warehouse, with a spreadsheet from a plane.

I gave a short speech. I don't remember what I said. Something about this being the first step, that we'd keep going, that this was just the beginning. Thanks to everyone who'd made it happen. Something like that.

What I was thinking was: we're alive. For now.

✦

Because the timing was terrible.

Two weeks earlier, Lehman Brothers had collapsed. The global financial system was having a heart attack. Credit markets froze. Companies that needed to raise money couldn't. Banks weren't lending. Investors were sitting on cash or losing it. It was the worst possible moment to need capital, and I needed it for everything.

SpaceX had made orbit, but SpaceX still didn't have a lot of revenue. Falcon 9 was a long way from flying. We had a few contracts. Not enough.

Tesla was worse.

✦

The Roadster was in production, slowly. But we were losing money on every one. The cost to build each car had exploded to around $140,000 at one point, and we were selling them for a little over $100,000. The more we sold, the more we lost.

The company had been badly managed in some areas. Not because people were stupid. Because building cars is incredibly hard, and a start-up trying to do it for the first time makes every possible mistake. The supply chain was a mess. The transmission was a disaster. We had suppliers in Asia, Europe, and America, with parts being shipped all over the world, and each step added cost and delay.

In October 2008, I took over as CEO. I'd been chairman and the main funder. Now I was in charge of operations. I hadn't wanted to do that. I already had a rocket company to run. But I'd looked at the options and none of them were good, and if I didn't do it, I thought the company would die.

The first thing I did was cut costs. We laid off about a quarter of the staff. That's a horrible thing to do. People who'd worked hard, believed in the company, done nothing wrong, and now they're out of a job in a recession. I hated it. But if you don't cut when you have to, you don't get to keep anybody.

We shut down an office in Detroit. We renegotiated with suppliers. We raised prices on Roadsters that were on order, which made customers angry, but we had to, because we couldn't keep losing money on each car. Some of those customers left. A lot of them stayed. I'm still grateful to the ones who stayed.

✦

The press was having a field day. Tesla was on lists of companies most likely to die in 2008. There was a website that tracked Tesla's death. Every day there was another story about the company running out of money, which it was. Every day there was another analyst saying electric cars were a fantasy.

The skeptics weren't crazy. They'd seen this before. Lots of car startups. All dead. Electric cars had been tried and failed for a century. The economics didn't work. Batteries were too expensive. The big companies knew how to build cars and they'd decided not to build electric ones.

That's a reasonable prior. I'd have agreed with it, in the abstract. My counter was just the battery cost curve. But a curve is not a balance sheet, and it was the balance sheet that was going to kill us.

✦

My personal life was also falling apart. Justine and I had separated earlier that year. We were divorcing. It was public, partly, because she wrote about it, which she had every right to do. I won't add to that. It was a painful time for both of us and for the kids.

I did meet someone. In London, in July 2008, at a party. An English actress named Talulah Riley. She was twenty-two. I was thirty-seven. I asked her out, nervously. We'd marry in 2010. That's its own story, and it's hers as much as mine. I'll just say that in the worst year of my life she was kind to me, and she made things less bleak.

✦

By December, I had to make a choice.

I had some money left from PayPal. Not much. I could put it all into SpaceX, which had just reached orbit and had a good shot. Or I could put it all into Tesla, which was on the edge. Or I could split it, and maybe both would die.

That's a classic decision theory problem. If you split, you might get two dead companies. If you choose one, you get one that probably lives and one that definitely dies.

I couldn't choose. Tesla was the electric car. If Tesla died, I thought it would set the cause back by a decade. People would say, see, electric cars don't work, even Musk couldn't make it work. SpaceX was the path to Mars. If SpaceX died, I thought it might be decades before anyone else tried.

So I split it. I put everything I had left into both. And I borrowed money to live on. I remember borrowing money from friends for rent, more or less. People find that hard to believe now. It's true.

✦

Tesla needed a financing round to close by Christmas Eve, or it would miss payroll and go bankrupt. Literally. The money would run out the next week.

We had a round lined up with existing investors. One of the major investors raised concerns and the round got stuck. I understood the concerns, in retrospect. They were looking at a company in a terrible market with a lot of risk and they wanted better terms. But at that moment it felt like the company was going to die over paperwork. The irony was not lost on me.

I said I'd put in the money myself. All of it, if necessary. Borrowed from SpaceX if I had to. That got everyone moving. The round closed on December 24, 2008. In the late afternoon. A few hours before we'd have bounced payroll.

Kimbal put money in too. A significant chunk of what he had, at a time when his own business had been hit by the recession. That's what I mean when I say I'd trust him with my life.

✦

Then, the day before, December 23, NASA called.

SpaceX had been competing for a contract to fly cargo to the International Space Station after the Space Shuttle retired. Commercial Resupply Services. We'd been in the running alongside some much bigger companies. I'd thought our chances were decent but not great.

The call was that we'd won. Twelve flights. About $1.6 billion.

I don't remember what I said. I've been told it involved the word love, said to a NASA official, which was probably not protocol. I was standing in the house on a call. I think I shouted. Nobody minded.

That contract saved SpaceX. It gave us the money and the credibility to build Falcon 9 and Dragon. It gave us a future. Without it, I think we'd have been gone in a few months.

So that's December 2008. The rocket works. The money is gone. The car company has a few hours of cash left. Christmas Eve, the round closes. December 23, NASA calls.

People say I'm lucky. In that month, I absolutely was. If either of those things had gone the other way, both companies would probably be dead. I'd have been a guy who'd made some money on the internet and then lost it all trying to build rockets and electric cars. A cautionary tale.

✦

Let me say something about luck, because I think it's misunderstood.

Luck is real. Some things you can't control. The timing of the financial crisis. The timing of NASA's decision. The salt air on Kwajalein. If you pretend those things didn't matter, you're lying.

But luck is also a function of how many shots you take. If you only launch once, your chance of success is whatever it is. If you launch four times, you get four chances. If you keep going, keep trying, keep iterating, the chance that you get a lucky break goes up. Persistence is a way to manufacture luck. It's not a guarantee. It's a better distribution.

I also think there's a cognitive trap people fall into, which is that they treat a good outcome as proof of a good decision. That's not right. Sometimes you make a good decision and get a bad outcome. Sometimes you make a terrible decision and get lucky. Putting all my remaining money into both companies in a financial crisis was, by any reasonable analysis, a high-risk decision. It worked out. That doesn't mean it was smart. It means it worked out.

✦

The day after the Christmas Eve closing, I slept for about fourteen hours.

When I woke up, nothing was solved. Tesla still needed to build cars it couldn't afford to build. SpaceX had a NASA contract, which was huge, but the money would come in milestones, and each milestone meant hardware that didn't yet exist. The bank balance was positive. Barely. Both companies still had to do the hard part.

But there's a difference between hard and impossible. The week before, it was impossible. Now it was just hard.

I remember thinking about how close it had been. A handful of people, a few signatures, a single phone call from NASA. If any one of those had gone the other way, two companies would have died within days of each other. Thousands of people would've lost their jobs. And the ideas, reusable rockets, mass-market electric cars, would have been set back by a decade, maybe more. Because the obvious lesson the world would have drawn was: see, it doesn't work.

That's the thing about failure in a new field. It doesn't just kill the company. It kills the idea for a while. Investors remember. Engineers remember. The next person who tries has to overcome the memory.

So when people ask why I pushed so hard, the answer is partly that. I wasn't just trying to save two companies. I was trying to keep two ideas alive long enough to prove them.

✦

I spent Christmas 2008 mostly in a state of exhaustion that I can only describe as being hollowed out. I'd won, sort of. But I'd been running on adrenaline for months, and when it stopped, there wasn't much left. I remember lying awake on Christmas night thinking: I don't know how many more of those I have in me.

The answer, it turns out, is more than I thought. But fewer than I'd like.

The window is closing. That was my state all the time, then. Every month: cash, runway, deadlines. You live at the edge of a cliff, and the cliff keeps moving.

The year after would be better. Mostly. But I'd developed a kind of permanent nervous system response. A part of my brain that never stopped counting how many weeks of cash we had. Even years later, when the companies were worth enormous sums, that part never really shut off.

I think that's probably the real cost of 2008. Not the money. The alarm that never stops.

Falcon 9

The next few years were a sprint with no finish line.

At SpaceX, we had to turn a small rocket that had worked once into a medium rocket that would work every time. At Tesla, we had to turn a car we were losing money on into a car company that could make money. Neither had been done before by a startup in the modern era. Both had to be done at the same time.

I'd split my weeks. A few days in Los Angeles at SpaceX. A few days in the Bay Area at Tesla. I'd fly back and forth on a small jet, which people criticised as an extravagance. It was the only way to be in two places every week. Commercial flights would've cost me about a day a week in airports. I did the maths. The jet bought me something like fifty extra working days a year.

It also meant I barely saw anything except offices, factories, and the inside of the jet.

✦

Falcon 9 was named for its nine Merlin engines on the first stage. One Merlin Vacuum on the second.

People asked why nine engines instead of one big one. The old school view was that more engines meant more things to fail. The Soviet N1 Moon rocket had thirty engines on its first stage and failed every time it flew. Four times. So the lesson people took was: many engines, bad.

I thought that lesson was wrong. Or at least incomplete. The N1 failed because the Soviets didn't have the computing or the testing to manage that many engines. They couldn't test the whole first stage on the ground. Each failure took the whole rocket with it.

With modern control systems and a lot of ground testing, many engines can actually be an advantage. If one fails, the others can throttle up and the flight computer can adjust the trajectory. That's engine-out capability. Commercial airliners have two engines for the same reason. You can lose one and land.

With nine engines, you can lose one, maybe two, at the right point in the flight, and still reach orbit. That actually happened on a flight in 2012. One engine shut down early, and the rocket still put its main payload where it needed to go. Without that, it would have been a failure.

There's another advantage to many small engines. Manufacturing. If you build nine engines per rocket, you build engines at nine times the rate. You get better at it faster. You find problems faster. You can automate more. The cost per engine drops much faster than if you built one huge engine per rocket.

And there was a third advantage, though I wasn't sure about it then. If you're ever going to land a rocket, you need to be able to throttle down a lot. One giant engine can't throttle down far enough to land a mostly empty stage. But you can shut off eight of nine engines and land on one.

We weren't ready to land rockets in 2009. I was already thinking about it.

✦

Falcon 9 flew for the first time on June 4, 2010, from Cape Canaveral, at a pad called Space Launch Complex 40 that we'd leased from the Air Force and rebuilt.

It worked. First flight, to orbit. The first stage roll was a little off. There was a small spin at the end. It didn't matter. We were in orbit.

I remember feeling something unexpected afterwards. Not euphoria, like flight four. More like relief, followed immediately by a feeling of, okay, now we have to do this a hundred more times.

That's actually how it feels at that point. Reaching orbit once is a stunt. Reaching orbit every time is an industry.

✦

In December of that year, we launched Dragon on its first orbital flight. A pressurised capsule, designed to carry cargo to the space station and eventually people. It orbited, came back through the atmosphere, deployed parachutes, and splashed down in the Pacific. First time a private company had recovered a spacecraft from orbit.

We'd put a secret payload inside. A wheel of cheese. A reference to a Monty Python sketch. I won't explain the sketch. If you know it, you know. If you don't, it's not that funny anyway.

Humour matters here. Spaceflight is extremely serious. People can die. Everything costs a fortune. If you don't laugh, the pressure becomes unbearable. A wheel of cheese reminds everyone that we're humans doing this, not machines.

The skeptics in this period were impressive people.

In 2010, there was a big debate about the future of NASA. The Obama administration had cancelled the Constellation program, which was going to send astronauts back to the Moon with new government-designed rockets. Instead, they proposed to rely on commercial companies, SpaceX among them, to fly cargo and later astronauts to the space station.

That spring, Neil Armstrong and Gene Cernan testified to Congress. The first man to walk on the Moon and the last. Both of them were skeptical of the commercial approach. Their argument, roughly, was that the companies were unproven, that human spaceflight was too important and too dangerous to hand off to entrepreneurs, and that America risked losing its capability for years.

I was hurt by that. Armstrong was a hero of mine. A hero of everyone's. He'd landed on the Moon with seconds of fuel left. If anyone had earned the right to an opinion on spaceflight, it was him.

But I want to be fair about it. Their concerns were reasonable. In 2010, we'd flown Falcon 9 once. We'd never flown a person. We'd failed three times out of four on Falcon 1. If I'd been in their position, having seen what spaceflight costs in lives and money, I'd have been skeptical too.

The answer to that kind of skepticism isn't to argue. It's to deliver. Show the hardware. Fly it. Fly it again. Over time, the evidence changes minds. Or it doesn't, and you were wrong.

I said publicly that I hoped one day they'd see it differently. I don't know if they did. They both passed away before we flew astronauts. I wish they'd seen it.

At Tesla, 2009 and 2010 were about survival, and then about the Model S.

Coming Back Down

Here's the reusability argument in one paragraph, which I made probably a thousand times between 2010 and 2015, usually to people who were nodding politely while thinking I'd lost it.

A Falcon 9 costs, round numbers, about sixty million dollars to build and launch. The propellant, the liquid oxygen and kerosene, costs maybe two hundred thousand dollars. The first stage is about seventy percent of the cost of the whole rocket. If you could land it and fly it again, say ten times, you'd cut the cost of a launch dramatically. Do it a hundred times and the cost drops toward the cost of fuel plus maintenance plus the second stage. Do the second stage too, and the cost of getting to orbit drops by a factor of a hundred.

That's it. That's the whole thing.

Everything else is just figuring out how to do it.

✦

Let me do the explainer properly, because people still underestimate this.

Think about what it means to have a reusable rocket versus an expendable one, in terms of capital. With an expendable rocket, every flight needs a new vehicle. Your factory's output is your launch rate. If you want to launch twice as often, you build twice as many rockets. The cost per launch is dominated by the cost of building the hardware.

With a reusable rocket, the factory's output is your fleet growth. Each vehicle can fly many times. Your launch rate is determined by how fast you can turn a vehicle around, not how fast you can build new ones. The cost per launch is dominated by refurbishment, fuel, and operations.

The difference is like the difference between a disposable camera and a real one. Or a paper plate and a ceramic one. Or, the one I always used, an airplane you throw away after every flight versus an airplane you refuel.

Now, you pay a price for reusability. Landing requires extra propellant, which you can't use to carry payload. Landing legs and grid fins add mass. The stage has to be tougher to survive re-entry and landing. So a reusable Falcon 9 carries maybe thirty or forty percent less payload than an expendable one. You give up some performance to gain a lot of cost.

Engineers trained in the old way often found that trade-off offensive. Mass is everything in aerospace. Giving up a third of your payload capacity seemed insane.

But if you look at cost per kilogram rather than kilograms per rocket, the answer flips. A rocket that carries thirty percent less but costs ninety percent less per flight is the obvious winner. It's just that nobody had built one, so nobody had the data, so the analogy-based reasoning won.

There's a deeper point. If you can make access to space cheap enough, entirely new things become possible. Large satellite constellations. Orbital manufacturing. Space stations that aren't $150 billion projects. And, eventually, a city on Mars. You can't build a city on Mars at a billion dollars a ton. You can at a few hundred dollars a ton. The difference between those numbers is reusability.

✦

We started with a test vehicle called Grasshopper. It was basically a Falcon 9 first-stage tank with one Merlin engine and some landing legs. We'd fly it up a little bit at our test site in McGregor, Texas, hover, and land.

The first flights in 2012 went a couple of metres. Then forty. Then a few hundred. By late 2013 we flew it to about 750 metres, hovered, and came back down onto the pad. The videos look fake. They're not. A tall white tube rising on a column of flame, stopping, then descending gently onto its legs.

People who saw the videos for the first time thought they were fake. That's a good sign. It means you're doing something that doesn't fit in people's heads yet.

✦

Then we had to do it from orbital velocity.

That's an entirely different problem. Grasshopper went a few hundred metres up and came down. A real first stage separates at something like two kilometres per second, Mach 6 or so, at about seventy kilometres altitude, heading away from the launch site. To land it, you flip it around. Fire the engines to kill the downrange velocity and head back. Survive re-entry at hypersonic speed. Steer to a precise spot. Then a final landing burn, and touchdown at walking speed.

All with a stage that's mostly empty. It's like balancing a pencil on your hand in a hurricane. A 40-metre tall pencil, falling at supersonic speed.

We added grid fins, which are lattice-shaped steering surfaces that deploy near the top of the stage, for control during descent. We added cold gas thrusters to flip it. We added landing legs that unfold at the end. We wrote guidance software that solved the trajectory in real time, a convex optimisation problem, recomputed many times per second.

Then we tried. A few times we tried landing on the ocean, no target, just to see if we could control it to the surface. It worked well enough. Then we built a landing platform: a big barge, an autonomous drone ship that could hold position in the Atlantic. We named the drone ships after starships from Iain Banks's Culture novels, which are some of my favourite science fiction. The first one was Just Read the Instructions. Later, Of Course I Still Love You.

January 2015. The first drone ship attempt. The stage made it to the ship and hit hard. Exploded. The grid fins had run out of hydraulic fluid just before landing.

April 2015. Second attempt. The stage came down nearly perfectly, then tipped over at the last moment and exploded. A throttle valve was slow to respond.

We were getting close. Each try was teaching us something.

✦

Then June 28, 2015. My birthday. CRS-7, a cargo mission to the space station.

Two minutes and nineteen seconds into flight, the second stage broke apart. The rocket disintegrated in the sky. Dragon fell into the ocean.

That was our first Falcon 9 failure. Eighteen successful flights before it. I'd started to believe, emotionally, that it would always work. That was a mistake. Rockets are never boring, even when they look like they are.

The cause took weeks to find. It was a steel strut inside the second stage's liquid oxygen tank, holding a helium pressure vessel. The strut was rated to hold a certain load, and the supplier had certified it, but some of them were faulty. One broke. The helium bottle shot upward, ruptured the tank, and the stage came apart.

The strut was supposed to hold about ten thousand pounds of force. It failed at a fraction of that. We'd trusted the supplier certification. After that, we tested every single one ourselves.

Lesson: you are responsible for every part on your rocket, even the ones you didn't make. The rocket doesn't care whose fault it is.

✦

December 21, 2015. Falcon 9 back to flight, with a new upgraded version. Eleven satellites for a company called ORBCOMM. And the first attempt to land the first stage back on land, at a landing zone we'd built at Cape Canaveral, on a site that used to be an old Atlas launch pad.

I was in the control room at the Cape for this one. Actually I went outside to watch. The rocket went up. Stage separation. The second stage carried on. Then a few minutes later, you could see a light in the sky, coming back. The first stage, burning its engine, falling toward us. There was a sonic boom, a double crack, like distant thunder.

The light got brighter, descended, slowed, and touched down. Right on the pad.

I ran inside. People were screaming the word USA, which I thought was a strange choice for an international team, but okay. Someone was crying. I was grinning so hard my face hurt.

It was the first time in history that an orbital-class rocket booster had come back from space and landed upright.

Later I went out to look at it. It was standing on the pad, slightly scorched, with ice still on parts of it. It looked like it had been through something. It had. And it was intact.

That stage is on display now, outside our headquarters in Hawthorne. When I'm having a bad day there, which is often, I sometimes go look at it.

✦

In April 2016, we landed one on the drone ship for the first time. More landings followed, on land and sea. By the end of 2016, people mostly stopped saying it was impossible. They switched to saying it wasn't economical. That's the next stage of skepticism. First they say it can't be done. Then they say it can be done but doesn't matter. Then, when it obviously matters, they say they always knew it would work.

That's not a criticism. That's just how new ideas spread. You can't expect people to update instantly. Evidence takes time.

✦

Then, September 1, 2016.

We were fuelling a Falcon 9 on the pad at Cape Canaveral for a routine pre-launch engine test. A static fire, two days before launch. It carried a satellite for an Israeli company called Spacecom, called Amos-6. Facebook was going to use it for internet access in Africa.

During fuelling, the rocket exploded. On the pad. No launch, no flight. Just a sudden fireball, out of nowhere.

Nobody was hurt. We'd cleared the area. But we lost the rocket and the satellite, and badly damaged the pad.

That one was terrifying, because at first we had no idea what had happened. There was no obvious cause. It was an extremely fast event. A few dozen milliseconds from normal to catastrophe.

It took months to find. The cause was very subtle. Inside the second-stage oxygen tank, there were those helium pressure vessels again, wrapped in carbon fibre. We were loading super-chilled liquid oxygen, colder than usual so it'd be denser. It was so cold that some of it froze into solid oxygen in the gaps between the carbon fibre and the aluminium liner. Solid oxygen plus carbon fibre plus friction is a fire. The vessel burst.

That was a failure mode nobody had seen before, anywhere. It was a combination of things we'd changed to get more performance. Lesson: when you push the edges, you find new physics. Sometimes you find it with a fireball.

We changed the loading procedure. We redesigned the pressure vessels. We were back flying in January 2017.

✦

March 30, 2017. SES-10. The first time an orbital-class first stage that had already flown to space flew again.

That booster had flown a cargo mission to the station the previous year and landed on the drone ship. We'd inspected it, refurbished it, and now it was going up again with a commercial communications satellite.

It worked. The satellite went to orbit. And the booster landed on the drone ship again.

I gave a short speech that night. I said it was a huge revolution in spaceflight. I believe that's true. If you look at the history of launch from 1957 to 2017, every orbital rocket was thrown away, except for the Space Shuttle, which was partially reusable but needed so much refurbishment that it barely counted.

Today, Falcon boosters fly twenty, thirty times. That's normal now. People have stopped noticing. That's the best possible outcome for a revolution. It becomes boring.

✦

This was also the period when we built the Gigafactory in Nevada, when we launched the Powerwall and the Model X, and when Tesla bought SolarCity. That was controversial. Tesla shareholders sued, saying I'd pushed the deal through to bail out my cousins' company. I understood why it looked that way. SolarCity was struggling with debt, and I was chairman and the largest shareholder of both. The court, years later, ruled that the deal was fair. But I'd still say that the controversy was legitimate. Conflicts of interest deserve scrutiny, even when the deal is good.

My argument was that Tesla was an energy company, not a car company. Solar plus batteries plus cars. The pieces should be together. I still believe that. Tesla's energy business is now one of its fastest-growing parts.

✦

The window is closing. By 2017, I had reusable rockets, a car that was the best in its class, a battery factory, a solar company. Not bad.

But Mars was still very far away. The Falcon 9 couldn't get anything meaningful there. We needed something much bigger.

And the Model 3 was coming. The car that was supposed to bring electric cars to the mass market. The third step of the secret master plan.

I thought it would be hard.

I was wrong. It would be so much worse than hard.

Production Hell

On March 31, 2016, we unveiled the Model 3 in Los Angeles. A thirty-five thousand dollar electric car with more than two hundred miles of range. The car that was supposed to make electric cars normal.

People had been lining up outside Tesla stores that morning to put down a thousand-dollar deposit, before they'd even seen it. Within a week, we had over three hundred thousand reservations. That's billions of dollars of potential revenue for a car that existed as a few prototypes.

I was thrilled. I was also, in a part of my brain I tried not to listen to, terrified. Three hundred thousand cars. Tesla had built, in its entire history up to that point, about a hundred and fifty thousand. We'd need to build more than that in the first year of Model 3 alone.

We'd have to build a car company within the car company, at ten times the scale. In about a year.

✦

Here's a technical explainer that I think is the most important idea in this book, at least for the industrial side.

The product isn't the car. The product is the factory.

Anyone can make a prototype. Prototypes are hand-built by skilled technicians, using parts that are machined one at a time. A prototype might cost a million dollars and take months. The hard part, the truly hard part, is building a machine that can produce the car, at high volume, at low cost, with high quality, every time.

That machine is the factory. And the factory is many times more complicated than the car. The car has maybe ten thousand unique parts. The factory has machines to make and move and join each of those parts, plus the software to coordinate them, plus the supply chains to feed them, plus the people to run them, plus the quality systems to check everything.

I call it the machine that builds the machine. And it should be designed with the same care as the product, or more.

There's a simple way to think about factory output. It's roughly equal to:

the volume of the factory, times the density of useful equipment and activity in that volume, times the speed of the production line.

That's it. Output equals volume times density times speed. Most car factories in the world are not very dense. A lot of floor space is aisles, storage, empty air above the lines. And they're slow. A typical line moves at a walking pace or less.

If you could make the factory twice as dense and twice as fast, you'd get four times the output from the same building. Ten times as dense and ten times as fast, a hundred times.

That was my thinking for the Model 3 factory. I wanted it to be what I called an alien dreadnought. A factory so automated and so dense and so fast that it would look like something from the future. Robots everywhere. Humans only where needed. Lines running at speeds no one had seen before.

That was a mistake.

✦

Not because the idea was wrong in principle. Because I tried to do it too fast, without enough testing, with technologies that weren't ready.

We over-automated. We built complex robotic systems to do things that humans could do more easily. Some of the automation worked brilliantly. Some of it didn't work at all. Robots trying to fit fiddly parts, getting stuck, needing to be reset. Conveyors that jammed. Systems that worked in the test cell and failed at full speed.

The worst was a big fibreglass mat that was supposed to be installed on top of the battery pack by a robot. It had been designed so a robot could do it. The robot couldn't do it well. It kept failing. Each failure stopped the line. After weeks of trying to fix the robot, someone asked what the mat was for. It was for noise reduction. Someone tested the car without it. No measurable difference.

We deleted it.

That's the lesson in one story. I'd been trying to optimise a process that shouldn't have existed. I'd tried to automate it before asking whether to delete it. That's backwards.

From that came a rule I've used ever since, in roughly this order. First, make the requirements less dumb. Every requirement has a person's name, not a department. Second, delete any part or process you can. Third, simplify and optimise what's left. Fourth, speed up. Fifth, automate. In that order. The most common mistake of smart engineers is to optimise something that shouldn't exist. And I'd made it, at scale.

✦

By late 2017 the Model 3 ramp was a disaster. We were supposed to be building five thousand cars a week by the end of the year. We were building a few hundred.

The battery module line at the Gigafactory in Nevada was the first bottleneck. Then the body line in Fremont. Then paint. Then general assembly. Every time we fixed one, the next one became the constraint. That's normal in manufacturing, actually. It's called the theory of constraints. The output of a production line is set by its slowest step. Fix it, and the next slowest step sets the rate. You go down the line, one bottleneck at a time.

The problem was that each one took weeks. And we didn't have weeks. We had commitments, a stock market watching, short sellers betting we'd fail, cash burning. Tesla was losing hundreds of millions a quarter.

I moved into the factory.

That's not a metaphor. I slept there. On the floor, under my desk, sometimes on a couch in a conference room. For months. Usually three or four hours a night. I went back to the house rarely. I'd walk the lines at two in the morning, find a problem, get the engineer responsible on the phone or out of bed, and work on it until it was fixed. Then the next one.

The reason was simple. If I was at the factory, I could see problems in real time and make decisions on the spot. If I was elsewhere, every decision took a day. A day of lost production was thousands of cars.

I also wanted the team to see that I wasn't asking them to do anything I wouldn't do. If they were working ridiculous hours, I'd work more. That's a leadership principle I believe in. Not as theatre. As fairness.

It was also, I'll be honest, partly self-punishment. I'd promised things I couldn't deliver. I felt I owed it.

✦

Some people at the time worried about my health. Reasonably. I was exhausted, emotional, irritable, saying things in public that I shouldn't have. I'd tweet at three in the morning. Some of those tweets were, frankly, dumb. A few were very dumb.

I gave an interview to the New York Times in August 2018 where I was more candid than I'd normally be about how hard that year had been. It was a strange interview. I got emotional. The coverage made it sound like I was falling apart.

I wasn't falling apart. But I wasn't fine. Nobody is fine after a year of sleeping on a factory floor. Your judgement gets worse. You make decisions you wouldn't make rested. That's just how a human brain works when you starve it of sleep. Mine's not special.

The tent.

By the spring of 2018, the general assembly line was still the constraint. We'd got paint and body up to speed, more or less, but final assembly was stuck.

We needed another assembly line. A normal new line in a car factory takes a year or more to plan and build.

We didn't have a building to put it in. The Fremont factory was full.

So we built a tent.

A huge temporary structure, the kind used for big events, set up in the parking lot. Inside it, we built a new general assembly line, mostly from scavenged equipment and simple tools, and much less automation. It took about three weeks.

The tent line was a symbol. A tent. At a car factory. People made fun of it. But it worked. It added a significant fraction of Model 3 capacity, and some of the techniques we figured out there, simpler, faster, more human, were better than what we'd built inside.

At the end of June 2018, in the last week of the quarter, we hit five thousand Model 3s in a week, plus Model S and X. The whole team was in the factory. When the last car rolled off the line, we celebrated in the parking lot, at dawn, with everyone exhausted.

I went home and slept. I don't remember how long.

Then I nearly destroyed it all with one tweet.

In August 2018, I wrote that I was considering taking Tesla private at four hundred and twenty dollars a share and that funding was secured. I believed, at the time, that I had the backing of a Saudi sovereign fund. I'd had conversations. I took them more seriously than I should have. The funding was not, in the sense that a regulator would require, secured.

That tweet moved the stock. The SEC sued me for securities fraud. To settle, I agreed to step down as chairman of Tesla for three years, pay a twenty million dollar fine, and Tesla paid another twenty million. I'd also need approval from company lawyers for certain tweets about the company.

I was angry about it at the time. I'm less angry now. I'd still argue about some of the details. But the broad truth is that I made a mistake. I said something about a public company's stock, impulsively, while exhausted, and I should not have. That's on me.

The broader point: when you're responsible for large things, careless words have consequences. Tesla had tens of thousands of employees and lots of shareholders. My sleep-deprived impulses were not supposed to be their risk. I've tried to remember that since. I haven't always succeeded.

The bright spot in that year was Falcon Heavy.

February 6, 2018. Three Falcon 9 first stages strapped together. Twenty-seven Merlins. At the time, the most powerful operational rocket in the world.

We'd delayed it for years. It turned out that strapping three boosters together was much harder than we'd thought. The loads are completely different. The vibration modes are different. We'd basically had to redesign the centre core.

I'd said beforehand there was a good chance it would blow up on the pad. I'd said if it got far enough off the pad not to damage it, I'd consider that a win.

For the payload, I'd decided to put my old cherry red Tesla Roadster on top. Mostly because a test flight usually carries a block of concrete, and that's boring. With a mannequin in a SpaceX spacesuit in the driver's seat, which we called Starman, and a copy of The Hitchhiker's Guide in the glovebox, with Don't Panic on the dashboard screen. And a tiny Hot Wheels Roadster on the dash, with a tiny Starman.

It launched. It worked. The two side boosters came back and landed side by side at the Cape, simultaneously. That image, of two boosters touching down together, looked like science fiction. People cried. I may have.

The centre core didn't land. It missed the drone ship and hit the ocean at about 500 kilometres an hour. It ran out of the ignition fluid for relighting its engines. Not perfect.

But the Roadster went into orbit around the Sun, with Starman driving. It's out there now, on an elliptical orbit that crosses the orbit of Mars. It will probably be out there for millions of years.

I like that. A car, a mannequin, and a book about not panicking. Drifting around the solar system. It seemed like the right message at the right time.

The window is closing. By the end of 2018, Tesla was making money. Not a lot, but it was. The Model 3 was the best-selling luxury car in America. SpaceX was launching more than any other company in the world.

And I was more tired than I'd ever been. Maybe more tired than I've been since.

I promised myself I'd sleep more. I'd work fewer hours. I'd delegate more.

Some of that even happened.

Crew

When the Space Shuttle retired in 2011, America lost the ability to launch its own astronauts. For nearly nine years, the only way for an American to get to the International Space Station was to fly on a Russian Soyuz from Kazakhstan, at a price that climbed to something like eighty million dollars a seat.

That always seemed wrong to me. The country that had landed on the Moon was buying rides from its former rival. I don't say that as a criticism of the Russians. Soyuz is an extremely reliable spacecraft. The engineers who built it are excellent. But it was a strange state of affairs.

NASA's answer was the Commercial Crew program. Companies would build spacecraft to carry astronauts, NASA would certify them and buy rides. We were selected in 2014, alongside Boeing. Boeing got a bigger contract. Fair enough. They had a lot more experience. We were the upstart.

It took six more years to fly.

✦

Flying people is a different thing from flying cargo.

With cargo, if the rocket fails, you lose money and satellites and a lot of sleep. With people, if the rocket fails, people die. Everything changes. The standards of evidence change. The level of paranoia changes. The weight on your chest changes.

Dragon for cargo had parachutes and a heat shield and could survive re-entry. Crew Dragon needed all of that plus life support, displays and controls, seats that could survive a hard landing, and, critically, a launch escape system. If the rocket fails on the way up, the capsule has to get away fast enough to survive.

Crew Dragon's escape system is a set of eight SuperDraco engines built into the walls of the capsule. If something goes wrong, they fire and pull the capsule away from the rocket at about four or five g in a fraction of a second. We tested it on the pad in 2015, and then in flight in January 2020: launched an empty Crew Dragon on a Falcon 9, deliberately shut down the rocket at the worst moment, maximum aerodynamic pressure, and let the capsule escape. The rocket blew up, as expected. The capsule flew away, deployed its parachutes, and splashed down fine.

In 2019 we lost a Crew Dragon capsule on a test stand when a valve failed and the SuperDraco system exploded during a ground test. Nobody hurt. The capsule was destroyed. It turned out that a small amount of oxidiser had leaked into a helium line and, under pressure, ignited a titanium valve. Titanium plus oxidiser under pressure can burn. We replaced the valves with a design that couldn't fail that way.

Each of these things set us back months. Each one was the right thing to have found before we put people inside.

✦

Some thoughts on responsibility, which I don't talk about publicly much.

When you put people on a rocket, you have to be willing to accept that you might kill them. Not in the abstract. In the specific. Here are the names. Here are their families. Here's the probability your analysis says it might fail. That number is never zero.

I'd sit with that number. NASA's requirement was something like a one in 270 chance of loss of crew for a mission. Our internal estimates were in that range or better. One in 270 is a small number. It's also, if you fly enough missions, a large one.

I used to ask myself: if this goes wrong, will I be able to say I did everything I possibly could? That's the standard. Not whether it works. Whether, if it doesn't, you'd be able to look the families in the eye and say there was nothing more you could have done.

I'm not sure anyone can ever honestly say that. But you have to try.

✦

Starlink started in this period too. The first sixty satellites went up in May 2019, stacked like a deck of cards inside a Falcon 9 fairing.

The idea was simple. Launch thousands of satellites into low orbit, a few hundred kilometres up, much closer than traditional communications satellites at 36,000 kilometres. Close enough that the delay is low, maybe twenty or thirty milliseconds, good enough for video calls and gaming. Cover the whole Earth, so anyone anywhere can get fast internet with a small dish.

That's an enormous engineering problem. Thousands of satellites, each one needing to coordinate with its neighbours, each one moving at about 7.5 kilometres per second, each one handing off connections to user terminals on the ground as it passes overhead. Then laser links between satellites, so data can route through space without needing ground stations.

It's also a business. Starlink was supposed to make money that would fund Starship and Mars. And, eventually, it did, and then some. It's now one of the biggest internet providers in the world. Ships at sea, aircraft, remote villages, disaster zones.

I'll be honest. I got a lot of things wrong publicly in the first months of that year. I predicted it would be over faster than it was. I was frustrated by the lockdowns, and in particular by the order that shut down our Fremont factory when other car plants in other states were allowed to keep going. I pushed back hard. Too hard, some people thought. Some of what I said was right. Some of it was wrong. I should have been more careful. Lives were at stake and I was sometimes glib about it.

But SpaceX kept working. The rockets kept flying. We were an essential business, launching national security payloads and supplying the space station. People worked in shifts, masked, tested.

And the first crewed flight was coming.

May 27, 2020. Demo-2. NASA astronauts Bob Behnken and Doug Hurley, in a Crew Dragon on a Falcon 9, from Pad 39A at Kennedy Space Center. The same pad that launched Apollo 11. The same pad that launched the first Space Shuttle.

Weather scrubbed it at the last minute. We tried again on May 30.

Liftoff.

I loved that. Regular people, not astronauts, in space. That's the point. Space shouldn't be only for a few highly trained government employees. It should be for everyone, eventually.

Thruster and helium leak problems on its first crewed flight in 2024. The two astronauts who flew it, Butch Wilmore and Suni Williams, stayed on the station for months and came home on a Dragon in 2025.

I don't take any pleasure in that. Boeing has done incredible things. The engineers working on Starliner are good people trying to do something extremely hard. Having two independent ways to get astronauts to space is good for everyone. Redundancy matters for countries as much as it matters for rockets.

Quick explainer. How a flat dish talks to a satellite moving at 27,000 kilometres an hour.

A Starlink satellite in low orbit crosses the sky in a few minutes. A normal satellite dish has to point at its satellite. You can't physically swing a dish that fast and that precisely, cheaply, on millions of roofs.

So the dish doesn't move. It's a phased array. Hundreds of tiny antenna elements on a flat panel. Each one transmits the same signal with a slightly different timing. Where the waves line up, they add together. Where they don't, they cancel. By adjusting the timing of each element, many times a second, you can steer the beam electronically. No motors. The beam tracks the satellite across the sky, then jumps to the next one as the first one sets.

Phased arrays used to be military technology. Very expensive. Fighter jet radars. The trick was making one cheap enough for a consumer to buy. The first user terminals cost us well over a thousand dollars each to make, and we sold them for less than that, at a loss. Then we did the usual thing. Cut parts. Redesign the chips. Increase volume. The cost dropped by something like a factor of five over a few years.

Then the satellites themselves started talking to each other with lasers. Light in vacuum travels about forty percent faster than light in glass fibre. So for long distances, say London to Singapore, data bouncing between satellites can actually arrive faster than data in an undersea cable. That's a strange result. Space is a faster wire than the ground.

The consequence: anywhere on Earth with a view of the sky can get fast internet. The ocean. The Arctic. A village with no road. That's about a third of humanity who'd been mostly offline.

And for Mars, eventually, the same idea. A constellation around Mars, linked back to Earth with lasers. Interplanetary internet. Slow by light-delay. Fast by bandwidth.

✦

The window is closing. After Demo-2, I had a feeling I hadn't had before. That we'd crossed a threshold. Private companies could launch people to orbit and bring them home. Reusable boosters were normal. Starlink was coming online.

So the question was: what's next?

The answer was the same as it had always been. Mars. And for Mars, Falcon 9 was not enough. Not even close.

We needed a rocket that could carry a hundred tons or more to the surface of another planet. Fully reusable. Both stages. Rapidly reusable, like an airplane.

We were already building it. In a field in South Texas.

Stainless

The southern tip of Texas, where the Rio Grande meets the Gulf of Mexico, is a strange place to build the most powerful rocket in history. Flat. Hot. Humid. Salt marsh and sand dunes and mangroves. A beach that goes on for miles with almost nobody on it. A few dozen houses in a tiny community called Boca Chica Village. Brownsville about half an hour inland.

We bought land there starting around 2012. The original idea was a commercial launch site for Falcon. That changed. By 2018 it was where we'd build Starship. In the open air, basically, at first. Tents. Shipping containers. A few big welding stations. Rocket parts lying around in the sand.

The local wildlife included alligators, rattlesnakes, and a lot of pelicans. And a growing number of people with cameras on the side of the road, filming everything we did, livestreaming it to the internet. I'd sometimes find out about a problem on our test stand from a YouTube channel before our own engineers called me.

I liked that, honestly. It's very hard to build a rocket in public. It forces you to be honest. You can't hide a failure when there are fifty people with zoom lenses watching.

✦

The biggest decision on Starship came in late 2018. Materials.

We'd been designing it in carbon fibre. Carbon fibre is extremely strong and light, the material of choice for high-performance aerospace structures. We'd built a huge carbon fibre tank, a test article, twelve metres in diameter. It was beautiful.

Then I looked at the numbers again.

Carbon fibre cost about a hundred and thirty-five dollars a kilogram, and building big structures out of it was slow and finicky. Lots of curing in giant ovens. Lots of potential for defects. And it doesn't love extreme heat, so you need a heavy heat shield on the outside for re-entry. And it doesn't love extreme cold either, at least not the way we needed.

Stainless steel, by comparison, costs about three dollars a kilogram. Thirty-plus times cheaper. And it has a property most people don't know about: at cryogenic temperatures, like the temperature of liquid oxygen or liquid methane, around minus 180 Celsius, some stainless steels get significantly stronger. Something like forty or fifty percent stronger than at room temperature. Carbon fibre doesn't do that.

Steel also has a much higher melting point than aluminium or the resins in carbon fibre. It can handle re-entry heat on the leeward side without any shield at all. You only need heat tiles on the side facing the hot plasma.

Add it all up. Strength at cryo. Less heat shield. Cost. Easy welding. Easy repair. A steel rocket ends up about the same mass as a carbon fibre one, for the same job. And it's dramatically cheaper and faster to build.

I made the call to switch to steel. Some of the team thought I'd lost my mind. Rockets are not made of steel. Not since the early Atlas, which had steel balloon tanks so thin they had to be pressurised to stand up.

It was the right call. Maybe the best engineering call I've made. You could build a steel rocket in a field, with welders who'd learned their trade on water towers and oil tanks. Which, in South Texas, is exactly what we did.

✦

Quick note on the engine, since Starship lives or dies by it.

Raptor burns liquid methane and liquid oxygen. Methane because it's cheap, clean, doesn't leave soot that clogs engines, which matters for reuse, and because you can make it on Mars. Mars has carbon dioxide in its atmosphere and water ice in its ground. With electricity, you can split water to get hydrogen, combine the hydrogen with carbon dioxide, and get methane and water. That's the Sabatier reaction, known since about 1900. You'd make your return fuel on Mars. That was always the plan.

Raptor uses what's called a full-flow staged combustion cycle. It's the most efficient cycle possible for a chemical rocket, and it had never flown before. Instead of throwing away the gas that drives the turbines, as Merlin's gas generator does, Raptor sends all of it into the main combustion chamber. Two preburners, one oxygen-rich, one fuel-rich, each driving its own turbopump. All the propellant ends up burned in the main chamber at very high pressure. Around three hundred bar, later more. That was a record for an operational engine.

It's ferociously hard. Oxygen-rich hot gas at those temperatures will burn almost any metal. Most engine programs that tried oxygen-rich staged combustion took decades. The Soviets were the masters of it. We had to invent new alloys.

Version one was hard to build and kept breaking. Version two was better. Version three, which flew in 2026, has most of the external plumbing and sensors integrated into the engine body. It looks almost bare, like something sketched rather than built. Much simpler, much lighter, much more thrust. That's the trajectory you want. Every version should have fewer parts.

✦

The first flying prototype was Starhopper, in 2019. It looked like a water tower with legs, which is roughly what it was. One Raptor. We flew it to about 150 metres in August and landed it a hundred metres away. It worked.

Then the big prototypes. The Starship test vehicles were numbered SN for serial number. SN1 through SN4 failed pressure tests or exploded on the ground in various ways. People called them the confetti series.

December 9, 2020. SN8. A full-height Starship, three Raptors, flew to about 12.5 kilometres. Then it did something no rocket had done. It shut down its engines, flipped onto its belly, and fell horizontally, like a skydiver, using four big flaps to control its attitude. A belly flop. Near the ground, it relit the engines, swung back to vertical, and tried to land.

It hit too hard and exploded. Pressure in the fuel header tank had dropped, the engines were starved, and it came down too fast.

But the flip worked. The belly flop worked. The data was all there. I posted congratulations to the team, and some people thought I was being sarcastic about a crater. I wasn't. That crater was worth more than a year of simulations.

SN9: crashed. SN10: landed, then exploded a few minutes later. SN11: exploded in fog. SN15, in May 2021: landed. And stayed in one piece.

Five tries. That's the rate of learning you get when you build fast and test fast. Each vehicle cost a few million dollars. Each failure told us something no simulation would have. If we'd spent two years analysing before flying, we'd have learned less, much slower, for more money.

That's the philosophy. Iterate on hardware. Make the cost of failure low enough that you can afford to fail often. Then fail often, and learn fast.

✦

April 20, 2023. Integrated Flight Test 1. The full stack: Super Heavy booster with 33 Raptors, plus Starship on top. About 120 metres tall. Twice the thrust of the Saturn V.

It lifted off. Several engines were out. It climbed slowly. Then it started tumbling. The stages didn't separate. After about four minutes, we triggered the flight termination system and it blew up over the Gulf.

I'd said beforehand that if it cleared the pad, that was a win. It cleared the pad. Just about.

The pad was not okay. The thrust of 33 Raptors pulverised the concrete under the launch mount and threw chunks of it hundreds of metres. That was my fault, partly. We'd known the pad was a risk. We'd been building a big water-cooled steel plate to go under it, and it wasn't ready, and I made the call to fly anyway because I thought the concrete would survive one launch.

It didn't. Lesson: don't let the schedule overrule the physics. I knew that. I did it anyway.

We built the water-cooled steel plate, which is like an upside-down shower head the size of a house, blasting water up into the exhaust. Second flight in November 2023. Hot staging worked: the ship lit its engines while still attached to the booster. Both were lost later. Third flight in March 2024: the ship reached nearly orbital velocity and was lost on re-entry. Fourth flight, June 2024: both booster and ship came down softly in the water, as planned. The ship's flap was half melted on the way down. It kept going anyway. I loved that.

✦

October 13, 2024. Flight five.

The plan was to catch the booster. Not land it on legs. Catch it with the launch tower.

The tower has two giant mechanical arms. We called them chopsticks. The tower and the arms together we called Mechazilla, which I think is the best name we've ever given anything. The idea was that the booster would fly back to the launch site, slow down, hover next to the tower, and the arms would close around it and hold it. No landing legs on the booster at all. Save the mass. Save the time. Put it straight back on the launch mount.

I was on the roof of a building at Starbase watching. The booster came screaming back from the edge of space, lit its engines over the Gulf, swung toward the tower, slowed, and hovered.

The arms closed.

It held.

There's video of the crowd's reaction from that day. Engineers in the control room screaming. People on the beach screaming. My own reaction was more like a stunned laugh. A thing seventy metres tall and on fire, caught by a building.

I've seen a lot of engineering. That's near the top.

✦

Why catch a booster with a tower instead of landing it on legs, like Falcon 9?

Three reasons.

Mass. Landing legs for something the size of Super Heavy would weigh many tons. Every ton of legs is a ton less payload, or more propellant to carry the legs around. If the tower does the catching, the booster doesn't carry legs at all. The tower stays on the ground, where mass is free.

Time. A Falcon 9 booster lands on a drone ship hundreds of kilometres out in the ocean, or on a pad some distance from the launch site. Then it has to be lifted, transported, inspected, moved back to the hangar, moved to the pad. Days. Weeks. A caught booster is already at the launch mount. In principle, you lower it back onto the mount, refuel it, stack a ship on top, and launch again. Hours.

Rate. If the goal is many flights a day, eventually, every hour of turnaround matters. The airplane comparison again: an airliner lands, taxis to the gate, refuels, boards, leaves, in under an hour. That's the target. A rocket that needs a week of handling between flights can never get there.

The cost is precision. The booster has to come back to within a few metres of a precise point in the air, at near-zero velocity, next to a steel tower it must not hit. The guidance has to be extremely good. The engines have to throttle and gimbal precisely. The tower arms have to move fast and absorb the load.

When people saw the first catch, they saw spectacle. What I saw was a turnaround time going from weeks to, eventually, hours. Less exciting to look at. Much more important.

✦

2025 was humbling. Ships lost on flights seven and eight, early in the year, from problems with the new upper stage design: vibrations, leaks, fires in the engine bay. Debris fell over the Caribbean. Airlines had to divert. That was unacceptable, and people were right to be angry. Then flight nine lost the ship on re-entry. Then a ship exploded on a test stand at Starbase in June, from a pressure vessel failure.

There were serious questions about whether we could make it work. Some of those questions were from smart people who knew rockets. Fair.

Then flights ten and eleven, August and October 2025. Both worked. Ships made it through re-entry, did their flip, splashed down on target in the Indian Ocean. Super Heavy did its job. We'd turned the corner. Or at least found the next corner.

The window is closing. In late 2025, I was thinking about the 2026 Mars window. I'd said we'd try to send uncrewed Starships to Mars in late 2026. I knew it would be extremely tight. The ship had to reach orbit. Propellant transfer had to work. Thousands of things.

It didn't happen. We didn't make the 2026 window. I'll be honest about that. It wasn't close.

The next window was in late 2028. Twenty-six months later.

That's the thing about Mars. Miss a window and you wait two years. The planets don't care about your schedule.

Bandwidth

I've worried about artificial intelligence for a long time. Longer than most people realise.

Not in a science fiction way. I don't think robots with red eyes are going to march down the street. I think the risk is subtler and bigger. If you create something that's much smarter than you, you'd better be very sure its goals are aligned with yours. Because if they're not, you won't be the one making decisions anymore. And you may not get a second try.

I'd talk about this at dinners in the early 2010s and people would look at me like I'd said the Earth was flat. AI was a field that had overpromised for fifty years. Neural networks had been tried and abandoned twice. The idea that a computer would ever really think seemed absurd to most people, including most computer scientists.

Then deep learning started working. Image recognition. Speech. Translation. Games. Every year the systems got better, faster than anyone predicted. The curve was exponential and most people were still drawing straight lines.

✦

In 2015, a group of us started OpenAI. Sam Altman, Greg Brockman, Ilya Sutskever, others. I put in a lot of the early money. The idea was a nonprofit research lab that would develop AI safely and openly, as a counterweight to the big companies, Google in particular, which had bought DeepMind and seemed to be pulling ahead.

I left the board in 2018. Partly because of a potential conflict, since Tesla was doing a lot of AI work for self-driving. Partly because I disagreed with the direction. Later OpenAI created a for-profit arm, took billions from Microsoft, and became, in my view, the opposite of what it was supposed to be. Closed. Profit-maximising. I've said that publicly, and taken it to court. They see it differently. People can read the filings and decide.

I'll say this much fairly. The people at OpenAI built remarkable technology. ChatGPT changed the world's understanding of AI almost overnight in late 2022. You can disagree with someone's structure and still respect their engineering.

✦

The deeper problem, as I saw it, was this.

Suppose we do build AI that's much smarter than humans. Even a well-aligned one. What happens to us? We become, at best, a kind of pet. Well cared for, maybe. But not relevant. Not in charge of our own future.

Unless we can keep up.

And that's where bandwidth comes in.

✦

Quick explainer. This is the core of Neuralink and I've given versions of it many times.

Your brain is extraordinarily powerful. About 86 billion neurons, maybe a hundred trillion synapses. It does things no computer can do, still, with about twenty watts of power.

But it's connected to the outside world through very narrow pipes.

Input is decent. Your eyes take in a lot of data, maybe on the order of ten million bits per second at the retina, although most of it gets compressed fast. Output is terrible. When you communicate with a computer, you're doing it with your thumbs, or your fingers on a keyboard, or your voice. Think about how much you can type per minute. A fast typist does maybe a hundred words a minute. A phone typist, much less. Speech is a bit faster.

Studies estimate that human output, in information-theory terms, is around ten to forty bits per second. Some recent work suggests the rate at which we consciously process information is around ten bits per second. That's absurdly slow. A modern computer communicates at billions or trillions of bits per second. The gap is something like a hundred million to one.

So here's the problem. Your brain has an enormous amount of thought going on, and you're communicating it through a straw. You have this rich, complex idea, and you have to compress it into words, slowly serialise it into a linear string, type it out, letter by letter. The person on the other end has to decompress it, and most of the nuance is lost.

Now put an AI on the other side, thinking at billions of bits per second. You're talking to it through a straw. You can't keep up. It doesn't matter how smart you are. The pipe is too small.

A brain-computer interface could widen the pipe. If you could read and write to the brain directly, at high bandwidth, you could, in principle, communicate with computers and maybe with other people at much higher rates. You could have a kind of tertiary layer: your limbic system, your cortex, and then a digital layer, working together.

That's the long-term vision. The near-term one is much more concrete and, frankly, more important for now: helping people with paralysis, blindness, and other neurological injuries.

✦

Neuralink started in 2016. A small team, mostly neuroscientists and engineers, in San Francisco. DJ Seo, who'd done his PhD on very small wireless implants, was one of the founders and later became president. The aim was to build an implant that could record from thousands of neurons, wirelessly, safely, with a surgery simple enough to eventually be done routinely.

The device is about the size of a coin. It sits in a small hole in the skull, flush with the bone. From it come threads, each thinner than a human hair, with many electrodes along each one. The threads are inserted into the cortex by a surgical robot, because they're too fine and too numerous for a human hand. The robot avoids blood vessels. The chip records spikes from neurons, processes them, and transmits wirelessly. It charges inductively, through the skin, overnight.

It took years to get that working in animals. We showed a pig with an implant in 2020, a pig called Gertrude, who did not care in the slightest about being famous. In 2021, a macaque monkey named Pager played Pong with his mind, using a joystick at first and then without one. People thought it was creepy. I understood that. I thought it was amazing.

There were also criticisms about animal welfare. Some of those were legitimate concerns and we took them seriously. Some were exaggerated. I'll say what I believe: animal research for medical devices is necessary, it must be humane, and we tried hard to make it so. When we got something wrong, we fixed it.

✦

In January 2024, the first human.

Noland Arbaugh. He'd been paralysed from the shoulders down in a diving accident in 2016. He was in his late twenties. Funny, smart, a big gamer. He volunteered to be the first.

After the implant, within weeks, Noland was moving a cursor on a computer screen with his thoughts. Then playing chess. Then playing Civilization late into the night, which seemed like a very human thing to do with a brain implant. He said in interviews that it had given him back some independence. He could browse the web, text, play games, without needing someone to help him.

There was a setback. Some of the threads retracted from his brain in the weeks after surgery, which reduced the number of working electrodes. Performance dropped. The team adjusted the software, changed how the signals were decoded, and his performance recovered and then exceeded where it had been. We changed the surgical technique for later patients.

That's the kind of problem you expect with a brand new medical device. You find it, you're honest about it, you fix it.

His cursor control set records for brain-computer interfaces. Around eight or nine bits per second at first, which sounds small, but it's comparable to a person using a mouse. Later patients went higher.

By September 2025, twelve people had implants. By January 2026, twenty-one. People with spinal cord injuries, people with ALS. One man with ALS who couldn't speak used it to edit and narrate a video of himself. Another designed 3D parts in CAD software with his thoughts. A trial started to control a robotic arm. Another for speech. And there's Blindsight, the vision implant, which received breakthrough device status from the FDA in 2024, and as I write about 2026, was behind the timeline I'd given. I'd said it would be in a human within months. It wasn't. My timelines, as I've mentioned.

The robot.

In August 2021, at Tesla's AI Day, I announced that we'd build a humanoid robot. We called it Optimus.

To introduce it, a person in a robot suit came out on stage and danced. I'll admit that was a bit silly. People laughed. Some thought it was a joke. A year later, in September 2022, we showed a real prototype walking slowly across the stage. Clunky. Bumblebee, we called it. It waved. People laughed again, a bit less.

By the end of 2023, the second generation could walk smoothly, pick up eggs without breaking them, do squats. At an event in late 2024, Optimus robots walked among the guests and served drinks. Critics pointed out that some of them were being teleoperated by humans. That was true. We should have been more upfront about it. The bodies were real. The autonomy wasn't finished.

Why build a humanoid robot? Because the world is built for humans. Every door handle, staircase, tool, car seat and kitchen is designed for a body roughly our shape. If you want a robot that can do any job a human can do, without rebuilding the world around it, it needs to be a humanoid.

And Tesla already had most of the pieces. A car with self-driving is basically a robot on wheels. It has cameras, a neural network that understands the world, actuators, batteries, a computer. Optimus is the same stack, rearranged into a different shape.

✦

The hand is the hard part. People don't appreciate this.

A human hand has about twenty-seven degrees of freedom, depending on how you count, driven by muscles mostly in the forearm through tendons. It can apply delicate force or crushing force. It can feel texture, temperature, slip. It's one of the most sophisticated mechanisms in biology.

To make a robot hand that's anywhere near as capable, you need many small actuators, tendons or linkages, sensors in the fingertips, and a control system that coordinates all of it. And it has to be durable. A human hand heals. A robot hand has to survive millions of cycles without breaking.

In 2026, the hands are still the limiting factor. Robots coming off the line can do tightly defined tasks well, but generalising, doing a new task they weren't trained on, and doing it reliably for months, is the remaining problem. The hands wear. The tendons stretch. The software doesn't handle novelty well enough yet.

But it's improving fast. Every version has more dexterity and fewer parts.

✦

So by 2023, I had three bets on the future of intelligence. Tesla's AI for self-driving and Optimus. Neuralink for keeping humans in the loop. And a new one: an AI company of my own.

✦

Quick physics. Why refuelling in orbit matters.

Starship, launched from Earth, gets to low orbit with its tanks nearly empty. It's burned almost all of its propellant to get there. The rocket equation is brutal, as I explained back in the Falcon 1 days. Getting to orbit takes about 9.4 kilometres per second, and that eats most of what you've got.

To go from low Earth orbit to the Moon, or to Mars, you need a lot more delta-v. Something like six kilometres per second to land on the Moon, more if you want to come back. About four kilometres per second to leave Earth orbit toward Mars on a reasonable trajectory, plus whatever you need on arrival, though Mars's atmosphere can do most of the braking for you.

So you need to fill up the tanks in orbit. That means launching tanker Starships, which carry propellant instead of payload, and transferring that propellant to the ship that's going to the Moon or Mars.

How many? Depends on the mission. For a lunar landing with a big payload, something like eight to twelve tanker flights. For Mars, maybe four to six per ship, depending on how fast you want to go and how much you're carrying.

That's a lot of launches. That's why reusability and cadence matter so much. If each tanker launch costs a few million dollars, eight launches is affordable. If each costs a few hundred million, it isn't.

The hard part of transfer is physics. In zero gravity, liquid doesn't sit at the bottom of the tank. There's no bottom. It floats around in blobs and clings to walls. If you open a valve, you might get liquid, or you might get gas, or a mix. You need to push the liquid toward the outlet. One way is to accelerate the two ships gently together, with small thrusters, so the liquid settles toward the back. That's called ullage settling. Then you can pump, or use pressure, to transfer it.

And the propellants are cryogenic. Liquid oxygen at minus 183 Celsius. Liquid methane at minus 162. In orbit, the Sun heats one side of the ship, and the propellant slowly boils. You lose some every hour. That's called boil-off. You need to manage it: insulate, shade, keep the transfer fast, and eventually add active cooling.

None of this is impossible. NASA studied it for decades. Nobody had done it at scale with cryogenic propellants, between two large ships.

The gauging sensors, which measure how much propellant is in each tank, disagreed with each other by a lot. In zero g, measuring how much liquid you have is surprisingly hard.

So we had a partial success and a lot of data.

Two: the cost of a robot isn't just its price. It's maintenance, integration, supervision, liability, and the cost of failures. A robot that fails one time in a thousand, in a warehouse, is fine. In a home with children, or a hospital, it's a disaster.

Three: humans are surprisingly cheap in a lot of the world. Quick explainer on why propellant transfer is hard.

On Earth, you pour liquid from one tank to another, and gravity does the work. Liquid goes down. Gas goes up. Simple.

If you take too long, you lose a chunk of it. So transfers had to be fast, and the tanks needed good insulation and shading.

Then there's the plumbing. Two ships, docked in orbit, with a connection that has to seal perfectly at cryogenic temperatures, carry a large flow, and disconnect cleanly afterwards.

Each piece is solvable. Together, they'd never been done at this scale. NASA had done small experiments. Nothing like moving hundreds of tons between ships.

Regolith

The ship we sent to the Moon in February 2028 didn't have a name. Just a number. Ship 58. The engineers called it Fifty-Eight, or the Moon ship, or occasionally things I won't print.

It was a lander variant of Starship. No heat shield, since it was never coming back to Earth. No flaps. Extra insulation. Landing thrusters partway up the body, so that the main engines wouldn't blast a crater under the ship and spray rocks in every direction. Landing legs, since there was no tower on the Moon to catch it. A big elevator to lower cargo to the surface, about thirty metres down.

Inside it: twelve Optimus robots. Deployable solar arrays. A small electric rover. An excavator about the size of a ride-on mower. A lot of batteries and cable. Science instruments for NASA. And a small box of notes and drawings from SpaceX employees' kids. I insisted on that one. Mass budget: 400 grams. Worth it.

Ship 58 launched in late January. It took eleven tanker flights to fill it up in Earth orbit. Eleven launches, eleven dockings, eleven transfers. In about three weeks. A year earlier, that would have seemed absurd. Now it was a logistics problem.

Then it lit its engines and left for the Moon.

✦

Why robots first?

Partly safety. If the landing failed, we'd lose hardware, not people.

Partly learning. A robot on the Moon tells you everything about what the Moon does to machines. Dust. Thermal cycling. Radiation. Communication delays. Every problem a robot finds is a problem a human won't have to find the hard way.

And partly because I wanted to show something. That the work of building on other worlds doesn't have to wait for humans to arrive and do it with their hands in spacesuits. Robots can go first and get things ready. Set up power. Dig. Build. So when humans arrive, they arrive at something, not nothing.

✦

Quick explainer: what the Moon does to things.

First, dust. Lunar regolith is not like sand on Earth. Earth sand is eroded by wind and water into rounded grains. Lunar dust has never been weathered. It's been pulverised by micrometeorites over billions of years into sharp, jagged, glassy particles. Some as fine as smoke. It's also electrostatically charged by sunlight, so it clings to everything. The Apollo astronauts found that it wore through the layers of their boots, scratched their visors, jammed the seals on their sample containers, and got into the cabin, where it smelled, they said, like spent gunpowder.

For a robot, dust is a disaster for joints. Every moving part needs a seal, and every seal is attacked by tiny glass knives.

Second, temperature. At the lunar south pole, the Sun is always close to the horizon. Some crater rims get sunlight nearly all the time. Some crater floors haven't seen sunlight in billions of years. In sunlight, the surface can be around a hundred Celsius or more. In permanent shadow, it can be minus two hundred and thirty or colder. Some of the coldest places in the solar system. Water ice survives there. That's why everyone wants to go to the south pole.

A robot walking from sun to shadow goes through a temperature change of hundreds of degrees. Lubricants freeze. Batteries stop working. Materials contract at different rates and crack.

Third, latency. Light takes about 1.3 seconds to get from the Earth to the Moon. So a round trip, from command to visible result, is at least 2.6 seconds, more with processing. You can't really joystick a robot with that much delay. It needs to be mostly autonomous, with humans setting goals rather than steering.

The robots we sent were a lunar variant. Sealed joints with layered dust barriers. Heaters. Thicker insulation. Radiation-tolerant computers. A slower, more careful walking gait. Bigger feet. They'd been tested in vacuum chambers full of simulated regolith on Earth. Simulated regolith is never quite the same as the real thing. We knew that. We'd find out how different.

February 2028. The landing.

The target was a spot on a ridge near Shackleton crater, at the south pole, chosen because it was in sunlight most of the time and close to permanently shadowed regions where we expected ice.

I watched from Starbase. Lots of people watched. Hundreds of millions, by the end. The video feed from the ship was a bit delayed and grainy at first. Then it cleared up as the ship descended.

The surface of the Moon at the south pole is grey and strange. Long shadows everywhere. The Sun barely above the horizon, so every boulder casts a shadow fifty metres long. It looks like a painting of evening. It's always evening there.

The ship came down slowly on its landing thrusters. You could see the dust blowing outward in perfectly straight lines, because there's no air to make it swirl. Each grain just flies outward on its own ballistic trajectory.

Touchdown. The legs compressed. The ship tilted, slightly, then settled. About two degrees off vertical. Within limits.

I remember the control room in Hawthorne being louder than I'd heard it since Demo-2. And I remember thinking, very calmly, oddly calmly: the first time a ship this big has landed anywhere other than Earth.

Then it stepped off.

The first two weeks went better than I'd feared and worse than I'd hoped.

Within four days, the robots had deployed vertical solar arrays on the ridge, about forty kilowatts' worth, tall masts with panels that could rotate to follow the Sun around the horizon. Vertical because the Sun is low. Tall because the light is better a few metres off the ground. They ran cables back to the ship and to a battery bank.

Within a week, they'd used the excavator to dig a trench about a metre deep and cleared and flattened an area for a future landing pad. That's important: if future ships land on bare regolith, they'll throw rocks into everything around them. A hardened pad stops that.

The dust was worse than the tests. Fine particles got into joints despite the seals. After about ten days, three of the robots had at least one joint that was sticking. We put up a small marker on the rim later. Not because they're people. Because they were the first.

Fleet

Mars and Earth line up favourably once every twenty-six months. That's not a schedule. That's celestial mechanics. It doesn't care about your launch readiness, your funding, your press release or your birthday.

Let me explain why, because it governs everything about going to Mars, and it's the most literal version of the phrase I keep repeating.

✦

Quick physics. The Mars window.

Earth goes around the Sun in one year. Mars takes about 1.88 years. So Earth laps Mars, the way a faster runner laps a slower one on a track. The time between laps, when the two planets are in the same relative position, is called the synodic period. For Earth and Mars, it's about 780 days. Twenty-six months, roughly.

The most efficient way to get from Earth to Mars is something called a Hohmann transfer. You leave Earth's orbit going a bit faster than Earth, on an ellipse that touches Earth's orbit at one end and Mars's orbit at the other. You coast for about eight and a half months, half an orbit around the Sun. You arrive at Mars's orbit exactly when Mars gets there.

For that to work, Mars has to be in the right place when you leave: about 44 degrees ahead of Earth in its orbit. That alignment happens once per synodic period. Leave a few weeks early or late and you need more energy. Leave months off and the trip becomes impractical, or impossible, with the propellant you've got.

So you get a window. A few weeks to a couple of months, every twenty-six months. Then it closes.

There are variations. If you have extra propellant, which a refilled Starship does, you can take a faster path, shorter than a Hohmann, three to six months. Good for humans, who'd rather not spend nine months in deep space getting irradiated. Or you can take a slower, longer path, sometimes called type II, that goes more than halfway around the Sun. That can lower your arrival speed at Mars, which helps with landing, or shift your arrival date to avoid Martian dust storm season.

And the windows are not equal. Mars's orbit is fairly elliptical, so some windows are much better than others. Roughly every fifteen or seventeen years, there's a very good one. The 2035 window, with Mars at close opposition, was one of the good ones. That will matter later.

The point is: miss a window, and you wait two years. There's no catching up. You can't work harder and make Mars come back sooner.

That's the hardest constraint I've ever worked under. Most deadlines are human. This one is orbital.

✦

The 2028 window opened in December and ran through about February 2029.

We'd planned five ships. All uncrewed, all cargo. Each one about a hundred and fifty tons of payload to the surface of Mars.

What do you send first? I spent a lot of time on that list. Here it was, roughly.

Robots. About forty Optimus units across the five ships. Mars variant. Better dust seals than the lunar ones, bigger batteries, better heaters. Much more autonomy, since the signal delay to Mars is three to twenty-two minutes each way, depending on where the planets are.

Power. Solar arrays, lots of them, flat ones that roll out on the ground. Mars gets about forty percent of the sunlight Earth gets, and dust storms can cut that down to almost nothing for weeks. So also a lot of batteries.

Propellant production. A pilot plant to make methane and oxygen from Martian carbon dioxide and water. The Sabatier reaction. If we could make propellant on Mars, ships could come home, and eventually humans could too.

Ice prospecting equipment. Radar, drills, small rovers. The landing sites were chosen based on orbital data suggesting ice not far below the surface, at mid-latitudes in the northern hemisphere.

Communications. A relay setup so the robots could talk to Starlink-style satellites around Mars and back to Earth.

Science instruments, for NASA and others.

Spare parts. Lots of spare parts. Whatever breaks on Mars, you fix with what you brought. That was the biggest lesson from the Moon: spares are not optional.

Over the next eight weeks, the fleet went out.

Each Mars ship needed about five tanker flights to fill up. Five ships, twenty-five tanker flights, plus the five ship launches themselves. Thirty Starship launches in two months, from Starbase and from the new pads at Cape Canaveral. That's a launch roughly every two days.

To put that in perspective: the entire world, in 2020, launched about a hundred orbital rockets in a year. We were doing thirty Starships in two months, each one far bigger than anything else flying.

There were scrubs. There was weather. A tanker had to be waved off from docking twice because a sensor was misreading. One of the pads had a damaged flame trench after a launch and was out for nine days. But the fleet kept going.

By early February 2029, all five ships had made their trans-Mars injection burns and were coasting toward Mars on their transfer orbits. We'd chosen the slower type-II trajectory for most of them, to arrive in early 2030, in a better season for landing at the target sites.

Five ships. On their way to another planet.

✦

Then we lost one.

Ship 71. Three weeks after its departure burn.

It started with a single sensor showing an unexpected pressure rise in one of the methane header tanks. The header tanks are small tanks inside the main tanks that hold propellant for landing, so that you've got clean, settled liquid when you need it. The pressure kept climbing. The relief valve should have vented it. It didn't, or not enough.

The team on the ground tried a series of commands to open the valve manually, cycle it, switch to a backup. The delay by then was over twenty seconds each way. Every command took close to a minute to confirm. It was like trying to fix a car by sending text messages to someone inside it.

After about four hours, the pressure was high enough that the system made a decision on its own: dump the header tank contents to protect the main structure. It did. That saved the ship from rupturing. It also meant it had no landing propellant anymore.

The ship was intact. It would get to Mars. But it couldn't land.

We'd have it do a flyby instead and take pictures. That's what happened. It's in orbit around the Sun now, on a long ellipse. A hundred and fifty tons of robots, solar panels, and parts, sailing past Mars and continuing on, forever.

✦

The root cause took about two weeks to find.

It turned out the relief valve had a seat material that, after weeks at cryogenic temperatures in vacuum, changed its properties slightly. It became stiffer. On Earth, in testing, we'd never held the valve at those conditions long enough to see it. In a few hours of testing it was fine. After three weeks in deep space, it wasn't.

That's a classic failure. Not exotic. You test what you think the environment is, and the real environment is subtly different, usually in the duration.

The other four ships had the same valve. That was the scary part. We didn't know if they'd fail the same way. We couldn't replace the valves; they were tens of millions of kilometres away.

What we could do was change the software. The engineers worked out a routine that would periodically cycle the valve and the tank pressure, gently, to keep the seat from setting. Like stretching a stiff joint. We uploaded it to all four ships. It worked. The pressures stayed normal for the rest of the trip.

So four ships out of five. Eighty percent.

✦

I want to describe how that felt, because people assumed I took it calmly. I didn't.

I was furious. Not at anyone in particular. At the universe, a bit. At myself, more. I'd pushed hard to get five ships into that window. Every one of them was overbooked, rushed, built faster than our usual pace. There's a real argument that if we'd sent four ships, carefully, instead of five, quickly, we'd have found the valve problem in testing.

I don't know if that's true. Maybe. I've thought about it a lot.

The thing I keep coming back to is: you can never find every failure mode in testing. Some failures only show up in the real environment, after enough time. The only way to find them is to fly. If you wait until you're sure, you'll never go, because you'll never be sure.

But you can be smarter about where you take risk. And I'm not sure we were, that time.

On Earth, 2029 was a strange year. The robots were spreading fast now. Several hundred thousand Optimus units were working, mostly in factories and warehouses, some in mines, some in agriculture. The Texas line was running. The hands were better. Not perfect. Much better.

Robotaxis were in dozens of cities. A ride was cheaper than a bus fare in some of them. People in those cities started asking why they owned cars.

Solar plus batteries had become the cheapest form of new electricity almost everywhere. Not by a little. By a lot. In sunny places, new solar with storage was undercutting the operating cost of existing coal and gas plants. The conversation had shifted from whether to switch to how fast.

Low resolution, like an early digital display. But they could walk through a door they couldn't see. Some of them cried the first time they saw their own hands as a pattern of light.

These things don't make the news as much as rockets. They matter more, day to day.

✦

People ask how you launch thirty Starships in two months. The honest answer is: the same way an airline runs a busy schedule. Boringly.

Each launch site had a rhythm. Booster caught by the tower. Inspected while still on the arms, mostly by cameras and robots. Lowered onto the launch mount. A ship, already inspected and fuelled with its payload or tanker load, lifted on top by the same arms. Propellant loading, about forty minutes. Launch. Then the cycle again.

The constraints were mostly ground systems. How fast you can make liquid oxygen and liquid methane and get it into the tanks. Each Starship stack takes about five thousand tons of propellant. That's a lot of cryogenic liquid. We'd built air separation plants at the launch sites, basically giant refrigerators that suck in air, cool it until it liquefies, and separate out the oxygen. Methane came from natural gas pipelines, liquefied on site. The propellant plants ran around the clock and were the real bottleneck for months.

Then weather. Then range safety. Then the human factor: people get tired. We ran the launch teams in shifts with strict rest rules, which I insisted on, given my own history with sleep. A tired launch director is a risk.

When it worked, it was dull. A launch every two days. People stopped watching. The press stopped covering each one. That's what success looks like in this business. When a Mars fleet launch is too routine for the news.

The window is closing. By the end of 2029, it meant something new. Four ships were approaching Mars. Their windows were the landing windows. You get one chance to hit the atmosphere at the right angle. Too steep and you burn up. Too shallow and you skip off into space.

The ships would arrive in January 2030.

I didn't sleep much that month.

Red

When a ship lands on Mars, you find out about it after it's already happened.

That's the strangest thing about it. In January 2030, the one-way light time from Mars to Earth was about eighteen minutes. So by the time the first signal arrived saying the ship had hit the top of the atmosphere, the ship had already been on the ground, or in pieces, for over ten minutes. Whatever happened, happened. You're watching history on a delay.

I knew that intellectually. I'd known it for twenty years. It was still weird to sit in a control room watching numbers that described the past.

✦

Quick physics. Landing on Mars.

Mars is the hardest place in the inner solar system to land something big.

On the Moon, there's no air. You come in, you fire your engines, you slow down all the way with rockets. It's expensive in propellant but conceptually simple.

On Earth, there's a lot of air. You come in at orbital speed, the atmosphere slows you down, you get very hot, and then parachutes or a landing burn do the rest. The atmosphere does most of the work.

Mars has air, but not much. About 0.6 percent of Earth's surface pressure. Enough to heat you up, enough to need a heat shield. Not enough to slow you down properly with parachutes, at least not for something heavy. Curiosity and Perseverance, at about a ton each, needed a supersonic parachute plus a rocket-powered sky crane. Starship, with a hundred-plus tons of cargo, is about a hundred times heavier.

So Starship's approach is to use the atmosphere as much as it can, then use engines for the rest. It comes in belly-first, the same as on Earth, using its heat shield and flaps. It skims the atmosphere at hypersonic speed, decelerating from several kilometres per second. That takes several minutes, with peak heating very high. Then, at low altitude and still going fast, it flips upright and lights its engines and slows down on rocket thrust the rest of the way, which is called supersonic retropropulsion. That's roughly what we'd done on Earth with Falcon 9 boosters for years, but on a different planet with a different atmosphere and nobody around to help.

The guidance software has to handle all of that alone. No human in the loop. By the time the data reaches Earth, it's long over. It has to know where it is, how fast it's going, how dense the air is, which way the wind is blowing, where the rocks are, and where to land. In real time.

Every number in that sequence had to be right.

Ship one, which the team had named Arcadia after the plain it was targeting, Arcadia Planitia, came in first on January 11, 2030.

Then touchdown confirmation. Then a picture.

The picture was grainy and slightly tilted, from a camera on the side of the ship, pointed down past a landing leg. It showed rust-coloured soil, small rocks, and the shadow of the ship stretching away across the ground.

Someone said, quietly, there it is.

Then the room went completely crazy.

So the robots inside had to get out another way. The team on Earth worked out a plan over two days, sending instructions with twenty-minute delays. The robots opened a side hatch, anchored a cable to a hard point inside, and climbed down it, hand over hand, one at a time, carrying their tools on their backs. Then they unloaded the cargo by hand, through the hatch, with a small winch, piece by piece.

It took about a month instead of a day. It worked.

I watched videos of that and thought about the boiler room in Vancouver. The robots weren't suffering. But they were doing ugly, improvised work that nobody had planned, because the plan had broken. That's what work on a frontier actually looks like.

The fourth ship landed on January 20, near the first. Perfect.

Four ships on Mars. Three on target, one close. Over five hundred tons of cargo on the surface of another planet.

The robots started work immediately.

The first job was power. They rolled out flat solar arrays across the ground, long strips like carpets, connected by cable to battery banks. Within two weeks, about two hundred kilowatts. Then more.

Then ice. The radar data from orbit had suggested ice below the surface at the site. The robots drove small drills. At about a metre down, they hit it: ice-cemented soil, then nearly pure ice. They brought up cores and put them in a small oven, and water came out.

Water on Mars, extracted by machines from Earth. Humans had seen evidence of Martian water from orbit for decades. This was the first time anyone had put it in a bottle.

Then the propellant plant. Carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, compressed. Water from the ice, split by electrolysis into hydrogen and oxygen. The hydrogen combined with carbon dioxide, over a nickel catalyst, at a few hundred Celsius. Out came methane and water. Recycle the water. Store the methane and oxygen as cryogenic liquids.

On March 3, 2030, the plant produced its first measurable methane. A few grams. By May, about a kilogram a day. That's tiny. A Starship needs over a thousand tons of propellant to return to Earth. At a kilogram a day, that would take about three thousand years.

But it worked. It was a pilot plant. The real plant would be a thousand times bigger. That's a scaling problem, not a physics problem. And scaling problems are what we're good at.

The dust storm came in July.

Not a global one. Regional. But it reached the site and darkened the sky for about three weeks. Solar output dropped by about seventy percent. The robots went into a low-power mode, which is a polite way of saying they huddled next to the battery banks and waited. They did a bit of maintenance work inside one of the ships.

That was a test of a question we'd worried about for years: can a robotic outpost survive a dust storm on solar power alone? The answer was: barely. With enough batteries and careful power management, yes. But it was much closer than I liked. One more week, and we'd have started losing robots to the cold.

I decided then that nuclear power on Mars wasn't optional. Small fission reactors, ten kilowatts to a megawatt. They don't care about dust. We'd been working with NASA's reactor program and private companies on small reactors. After July 2030, that program became a priority.

The reaction on Earth was the biggest I'd ever seen. Bigger than the Moon in 2028. Maybe because it was Mars. Maybe because of the robots climbing down the cable. Those videos were everywhere.

By some estimates, more than three billion people watched at least some of the landings live or near live. That's a number that's hard to verify, but it was a lot.

Kids. Lots of kids. Drawings of robots on Mars. Schoolteachers sending me letters, actual letters, with drawings inside.

Scientists, thrilled. Then, a few weeks later, worried. Planetary protection was a real concern. If there's any life on Mars, even microbial, even buried deep, we want to find it before we contaminate it with Earth life. We'd sterilised the cargo, mostly, but robots aren't sterile. People aren't either, and people were coming. This became a big debate, and it should have been. We worked with NASA and the international science community on zones: some regions designated for science only, some for settlement. Imperfect, but better than nothing.

Governments. The US government was proud and, again, uneasy that it was a company. The Chinese government announced an acceleration of its Mars program, including a crewed mission target in the late 2030s. Europe, India, Japan, the UAE all made statements, some with new funding. A big question at the UN was about what the Outer Space Treaty meant for Mars, given that one company was now operating machines on its surface. Who governed Mars? The treaty says no nation can claim sovereignty. But it says nothing about what happens when people start living there.

Protesters. Some people argued, sincerely, that we should be fixing Earth before going to Mars. I understood that. I'd answer it this way: the amount of money spent on Mars was a tiny fraction of global spending. Less than one percent of what the world spends on, say, cosmetics, or video games, in a year. And a lot of the technology developed for Mars ends up helping Earth: solar, batteries, water recycling, robotics. I don't think it's either-or. I think we can do both. Most people who've thought about it agree. Some don't, and they're entitled to that.

Religious leaders. Some of them said things I found moving, about the expansion of the light of consciousness, about humility before creation. Some were troubled. I respect both.

A woman wrote to me, an email, a few weeks after the landings. She'd worked on the Apollo programme as a young engineer, a computer programmer, one of the few women in the room back then. That's the best review I've ever got.

Quick explainer. Why landing on Mars is harder than landing on the Moon or Earth.

On the Moon, there's no atmosphere. You use rockets the whole way down. Simple, in principle. You just need enough propellant.

On Earth, there's a thick atmosphere. You can use it to slow down: a heat shield takes most of the energy, then parachutes or wings, or in our case the belly-flop and a short landing burn.

Mars is in between, which is the worst of both. The atmosphere is about one percent as thick as Earth's at the surface. Thick enough that you heat up hard coming in at several kilometres per second, so you need a heat shield. Too thin to slow a heavy ship enough with parachutes. So a large lander arrives near the ground still moving fast, and needs rockets to finish the job.

For Starship, the sequence went: enter the atmosphere at about seven and a half kilometres per second, nose up, belly first, heat shield taking the plasma. Hands

By the end of 2030 there were about a million and a half Optimus robots working on Earth. By the end of 2031, about six million. By the end of 2032, over twenty million.

Those numbers are approximate. Some of them were ours. By then, other companies were building humanoid robots too, in China especially, where the manufacturing base was enormous and the government had made robotics a national priority. Competition was ferocious. Prices fell fast.

I'd predicted something like this for years. I'd said it would be the biggest product in history. I'd said it would transform the economy.

What I hadn't fully thought through, honestly, was what it would feel like to the people whose jobs it changed.

✦

Quick economics. The cost of a robot-hour.

Let's say a humanoid robot costs twenty-five thousand dollars, which was about where the price settled for a mid-range unit in 2031. Let's say it lasts five years with maintenance, and works twenty hours a day, since it doesn't sleep, but needs time to charge and be serviced. That's about thirty-six thousand working hours.

Capital cost per hour: about seventy cents.

Power: it draws maybe half a kilowatt when working. At five cents per kilowatt-hour, which was a normal price for solar-heavy grids by then, that's two or three cents an hour.

Maintenance, software, insurance: call it fifty cents to a dollar an hour.

Total: somewhere around one to two dollars an hour.

Now compare that to human labour. In the US, a warehouse worker cost an employer maybe twenty-five to thirty-five dollars an hour, with benefits. In China, maybe eight. In India, maybe two or three. In much of Africa, less.

So in rich countries, the robot was ten to thirty times cheaper per hour. Even in poorer countries, it was competitive. And it didn't get tired, didn't get injured, didn't need to be trained twice, could work in the cold or the dark, and every robot got smarter every week via software updates.

The consequence is simple and enormous. Any physical task that a robot can do reliably will, over time, be done by robots. The price of that task falls toward the cost of the robot-hour, plus energy and materials.

That's deflationary. Goods get cheaper. Services that involve physical work get cheaper. Over time, a lot cheaper.

That's the abundance argument. I believe it. I also believe it has a transition problem. And the transition was where the pain was.

✦

The first big wave was warehouses, logistics, and manufacturing. Then agriculture, especially harvesting, which had been hard to automate because fruit is delicate and irregular. Then construction, slowly, starting with the dangerous jobs: working at height, demolition, heavy carrying. Then cleaning. Then, much more cautiously, care work: helping elderly people get out of bed, carrying things, fetching, cooking simple meals.

Trucking was hit at the same time by autonomous vehicles. By 2031, there were long-haul routes in the US where most of the trucks had nobody inside. The drivers, many of them, were in their fifties. They'd done that job for thirty years. It paid well. There wasn't an obvious next one.

I'd said for a long time that the right answer was something like a universal high income. Not basic. High. If robots and AI make goods and services extremely cheap, then society can afford to give everyone enough to live well. The question isn't affordability. It's mechanism. Who pays, how, and what happens to people's sense of purpose.

That's easy to say from a stage. It's harder to say in a break room in Fremont.

Dana Okafor ran the largest logistics workers' union in the country by 2031. Very funny, in a dark way. She'd say things in interviews like, the robots don't need a union, but the people who got replaced by them do.

In the summer of 2031 there was a wave of strikes and protests. People called it the robot summer. Port workers in several countries. Warehouse workers. Some farmworkers. In a few places, robots were vandalised. A Tesla factory in Germany had a blockade for a week. There were big marches in Washington and Brussels and Seoul.

Dana's union led a lot of the American side. Her demands were reasonable, I thought. Not stop the robots, which she knew was impossible. Instead: income support for displaced workers that lasted long enough to actually matter, not six months. Real ownership, not retraining slogans. A share of the productivity gains.

She asked to meet. I said yes.

✦

We met in a break room at the Fremont factory, at her suggestion. I think she wanted me somewhere that felt like work, not a boardroom. Plastic chairs. A vending machine. A robot walking past the window every few minutes with a crate.

She was blunter than any investor I've met. I said she was right about the order. I said the gains were real, and the question was how to share them.

She asked: then why isn't Tesla sharing them?

It was a good question. Tesla was making a lot of money from robots. Shareholders were doing well. The people whose jobs were automated weren't, by and large, shareholders.

I didn't have a great answer in that room. I said I'd think about it. She said she'd heard that before.

What came out of it, over the following year, was something we called the Transition Compact. It was negotiated with Dana's union and a few others, and it applied to companies that leased or bought our robots at scale, at least the ones that agreed to it.

The basic idea: when a company replaced a worker with a robot, the worker didn't just get severance. They got a stake. A share of the robot's productivity, paid out monthly, for a number of years. Funded partly by the company that deployed the robot, partly by Tesla from robot revenue.

It wasn't perfect. Critics on the left said it was too little. Critics on the right said it was a tax by another name. Economists argued about the incentives. Dana herself called it a down payment, not a solution.

But it was something. And it set a template that a lot of governments later borrowed for broader programmes.

✦

The broader programmes came in different shapes in different places.

Some countries did a straightforward robot tax: companies paid a levy per robot, and the money went to a fund that paid citizens a dividend. A bit like Alaska's oil fund, but for automation. I was skeptical of that at first. A robot tax slows down adoption, which slows down the price declines that make everything cheaper. But the political reality was that some kind of sharing was necessary. If people don't feel the gains, they'll stop the technology. Reasonably.

Some countries did a universal income directly from general revenue, which was booming, since productivity was booming.

Some did both. Some did neither and had a much rougher time.

By the mid-2030s, in most rich countries, there was some kind of floor. Not luxurious. But enough. Rent and food and electricity were getting cheaper, so the floor kept rising in real terms, even when the nominal payment stayed flat.

Now the slip.

November 2030. A software update for Optimus, pushed to robots working in warehouses and a pilot group of home robots.

The update included a new version of the walking controller, which made the robots faster on stairs. It had been tested extensively in simulation and in our own facilities. It passed.

In the field, within about a week, a pattern emerged. On certain types of stairs, with a particular combination of step height, lighting and a reflective surface, the robots misjudged the edge of the step. Some stumbled. Most caught themselves. A few fell.

One of them fell in a warehouse in Ohio, down a short flight of metal stairs, onto a worker coming up. The worker's arm was broken. Another fell in a home in the pilot programme. Nobody was hurt there, but a child saw it happen and was badly frightened. The family posted the video. It spread everywhere.

We rolled back the update to all robots within hours. Then we paused all home deployments and stair-climbing in all commercial deployments until we understood it.

The cause was a perception bug. The new controller relied more heavily on one camera's depth estimate when stepping down, and that estimate was confused by reflective edges in certain lighting. In simulation, the reflections weren't modelled accurately. In our own facilities, the stairs were all the same type. The real world had more variety than we'd tested.

✦

That incident changed how we deployed software to robots. Permanently.

A car update can be risky too, but a car has a driver, or at least has been tested for a very long time on roads. A robot in a home is in an uncontrolled environment, around children, pets, elderly people.

We changed three things.

First, staged rollouts with shadow mode. A new controller would run in the background on robots in the field for weeks, making decisions but not acting on them, and we'd compare its decisions to the existing version. Only when it was clearly better across many real-world situations would it go live, and then only on a small fraction first.

Second, hard safety envelopes. Certain behaviours, like how fast a robot could move near a person, or whether it could climb stairs when someone was below, were locked behind a separate, simpler control layer that the main AI couldn't override.

Third, an independent safety board, outside Tesla, with real power to stop deployments. Some of my own people hated that. They thought it would slow us down. It did, slightly. It also, I'm fairly sure, prevented a much worse incident later.

The US government created a new regulatory office for robot safety in 2031, partly in response. I'd generally been skeptical of new regulators. In this case, I supported it. Robots in homes need rules.

I visited the worker in Ohio. He was understandably not thrilled to see me. I apologised. He accepted it, sort of. He's back at work now, I think, supervising robots instead of carrying boxes. He told me once that his arm aches when it rains. I don't have a fix for that.

✦

There's a deeper issue that the robot summer raised, and I want to be honest about it.

Work isn't just income. It's identity, structure, community, meaning. A lot of people get their sense of who they are from what they do. If you take away the work and give them the money, you've solved one problem and maybe created another.

I've thought about this a lot. I don't have a complete answer. I don't think anyone does.

My partial answer is that when people don't need to work to survive, many of them find other things to do that matter to them. Art. Caring for family. Building things. Learning. Sport. Community. Some of the happiest people I know are people who don't have to work and choose to anyway, on what they care about.

But some people struggle without the structure. That's real. And it's not something a robot or an income payment fixes.

Dana said something to me, the second time we met, that I've repeated since. She said the question isn't what people will do for money. It's what they'll do for each other.

I think she's right.

Quick explainer. Why the hand is the hardest part of a humanoid robot.

The human hand has twenty-seven bones and is driven by more than thirty muscles, most of them in the forearm, pulling on tendons. It has something like twenty-plus degrees of freedom. It can crack an egg, thread a needle, swing a hammer and hold a child's hand. Same hardware.

Most industrial robot grippers have one degree of freedom. Open, close. That's fine for picking up the same part a million times. Useless for a world built for human hands: door handles, tools, cables, laundry, a coffee cup.

So Optimus needed a hand with roughly human dexterity, strong enough to do real work, light enough not to make the arm heavy, and durable enough to last years. Those requirements fight each other. More actuators means more dexterity and more weight and more things to break. Putting the actuators in the forearm, like a human, saves weight in the hand but means tendons, which stretch and wear.

Then there's sensing. Your fingertips can feel the texture of paper. A robot needs touch sensors in the fingers and palm to know how hard it's gripping. Too soft, you drop the glass. Too hard, you break it.

The hand ended up being about half the engineering effort for the whole arm. I'm not exaggerating much. The legs, the torso and the battery were hard. The hand was the problem we kept coming back to, for years.

When people asked how they'd know if humanoid robots were real, I'd say: watch the hands. If it can fold a fitted sheet, it's real. Most humans can't do that, to be fair.

✦

The window is closing. In this context, it meant: the transition has to be managed well, or people will stop it. Democracies can vote to slow down technology. They have, in the past. If the gains aren't shared fast enough, the backlash could stall the whole thing for decades.

That would be a tragedy. Not for me. For the billions of people for whom cheap robots mean food, housing, care, a decent life.

So I spent more time on this than I expected. More time in rooms with union leaders and politicians and economists. Priya Raman, the economist from 2027, was one of the people advising governments on transition design. We argued again. She told me I'd been half right. I told her she'd been half right too.

It was a good argument. We both knew it was the important one.

Tomás Reyes was a high school physics teacher in Arizona who developed ALS in his forties. By 2029, he couldn't move anything except his eyes, and that was going. He'd used an eye-tracking system to communicate, one letter at a time. Maybe five or six words a minute on a good day. He'd say, by typing, that it felt like shouting through a keyhole.

He got a Neuralink implant in early 2030, in the speech trial. The implant sat over the part of his motor cortex that controls the muscles of speech: lips, tongue, jaw, larynx. When Tomás tried to speak, even though his muscles couldn't respond, the neurons still fired. The device recorded them. A decoder translated the patterns into phonemes, then words. A voice synthesiser, trained on old recordings of his voice from school videos, spoke them.

About a month after surgery, at a session with the research team, he said a sentence out loud, in his own voice, for the first time in three years.

I'm not going to tell you what the sentence was. It was for his wife. It's theirs.

By the end of that year, he was speaking at about a hundred and forty words a minute. Normal conversation speed. He went back to teaching, part time, over video. His students said he made them laugh more than their other teachers.

When people ask me why Neuralink matters, I tell them about Tomás.

Quick explainer. Bandwidth, revisited.

Back in 2024, the first patients controlled a cursor at about eight or nine bits per second, as I mentioned. By 2030, cursor control was well past twenty. Speech decoding was more like a hundred bits per second or more, measured in information-theory terms, because language is information-dense. That's around normal human speaking rate.

How did it go up so fast? Three things.

More channels. The first implant had about a thousand electrodes. By 2030, the standard device had about three thousand, and a high-channel version had over ten thousand. More electrodes means more neurons recorded, more detailed signal, better decoding.

Better decoders. This was the biggest factor. The early decoders were trained on each patient individually, from scratch. By the late 2020s, we were training large models on tens of thousands of hours of neural data from many patients. Then fine-tuning them on each new patient. Same principle as language models. A decoder that has seen many brains learns the general structure of how motor cortex encodes intention. Then it only needs a little data to adapt to a new person.

The brain adapts too. That's the part people underestimate. The brain is plastic. When a patient uses an implant, their neurons change how they fire to make the decoder's job easier. The human and the machine learn each other. It's a partnership. After a few months, patients describe it not as thinking commands, but as just doing things. The way you don't think about moving your hand. You just move it.

Reading is one direction. Writing, stimulating neurons to create sensations, is harder. That's what Blindsight does: it stimulates the visual cortex to create points of light called phosphenes. Each electrode, roughly, makes a point. More electrodes, more points. Arrange them right and you get an image, low resolution, like the earliest digital displays.

Grace Adeyemi lost her sight in her twenties from an optic nerve condition. By 2030 she'd been blind for fifteen years. She was a lawyer in London. She'd learned braille, used a cane and a guide dog, and was, by her own account, completely fine, thank you.

She agreed to a Blindsight implant partly out of curiosity. She said in an interview that she didn't need to be fixed, but she wanted to see what it was like.

The first version gave her a grid of a few hundred points. Through a camera in a pair of glasses, the system translated the world into a pattern of light in her visual cortex. At first it was mostly noise. Then, after weeks of training, she could make out the edges of a doorway. Then the shape of a person. Then large letters.

She said that the first time she saw the shape of her guide dog, as a cluster of points moving next to her, she laughed so hard she had to sit down.

By 2033, with a newer implant, she had something like ten thousand points. Still low resolution. Like looking at the world through a very coarse screen. But she could walk a city street without the cane, read a menu if the print was big, recognise faces of people she knew, roughly. She kept the dog anyway. She said the dog was better company than the implant.

Three patients out of roughly six hundred at that point. A small number. But in medicine, especially brain surgery, small numbers matter enormously. If the rate was real, and you scaled to millions of people, that's thousands of serious injuries.

The FDA put a hold on new implants. We'd have done it ourselves if they hadn't. All new surgeries stopped. All existing patients were called in for imaging.

I want to describe what I felt, because it was similar to Ship 71 but worse. A ship is a machine. These were people who had trusted us with their brains. Two of them were people I'd met. One of them had a daughter who'd written me a thank-you letter the year before.

The fix took seven months.

A new thread coating, tested much longer in animals, with better long-term stability. Wider safety margins on blood vessel avoidance in the surgical robot, guided by better pre-surgery imaging. A protocol for regular follow-up imaging for all patients, so any early signs could be caught. And an independent registry, run by an outside medical institution, where every adverse event across every patient was recorded and made public, with patient consent.

Public. That part was important to me. The temptation, when something goes wrong with a medical device, is to manage the information. Release it slowly. Frame it. I've seen companies do that. I've probably been tempted.

But with brain implants, trust is everything. If people think you're hiding something, they won't get implanted. If they don't get implanted, the technology doesn't help anyone. So you put everything out there. Bad news included.

The FDA lifted the hold in October 2031. The trial resumed with the new devices and protocols. The complication rate after that was, over the following years, much lower. Not zero. Nothing in medicine is zero. Low enough that patients, informed of the risks, were choosing it in large numbers.

The patient who'd had the bleed got the new device in his other hemisphere a year later, by his own choice. He said that the year without the implant had been worse than the stroke.

And then there was the thing that, I'll admit, I'd been thinking about since 2016.

Two implant users, in the speech trial, both in the same research centre, did an experiment in 2033. Their decoders translated their intended speech into text. Normally that text would go to a voice synthesiser. Instead, the team routed it directly, as text, to the other person's implant interface, where it showed up as words on a display in their visual field, or as synthesised speech in an earpiece.

So the two of them could have a conversation without either of them speaking or typing. One would think the sentence, intend to say it, and the other would receive it, almost instantly. Faster than talking, because the decoder could pick up intent before the full sentence was formed.

It wasn't telepathy in the science fiction sense. They weren't reading each other's minds. They were each choosing what to say. It was just a much faster channel.

But it was a first step. A channel from one brain to another, with nothing in between but software.

The two of them, by the way, mostly used it to make jokes about the researchers. Which seems right.

Quick explainer. How a speech decoder works, roughly.

The implant records from a few thousand points in the part of the motor cortex that controls the lips, tongue, jaw and larynx. When you try to speak, even if your muscles can't move, those neurons still fire in patterns. Each sound you try to make, each phoneme, has a pattern.

The decoder is an AI model. It takes the neural signals, a few hundred times a second, and predicts which phonemes the person is trying to say. Then a language model, much like the ones that power chatbots, takes those phonemes and figures out the most likely words and sentences. Then a voice synthesiser speaks them, ideally in the person's own voice, trained from old recordings.

The tricky part is that the brain changes. Neurons shift slightly relative to the electrodes. Signals drift over hours and days. The decoder has to adapt continuously, quietly, without the user having to retrain it every morning.

When it works, the user just tries to talk, and the words come out of a speaker. A small delay. Tomás's first sentences took a few seconds. By the end of the first year, the delay was under a second, and he was speaking at over a hundred words per minute.

Normal conversation is about a hundred and fifty. Close enough to argue with your family at dinner. Which he did.

If humans were going to stay in the loop, not just as users of AI but as partners, the bandwidth had to keep going up. A lot. Hundreds of bits per second wasn't enough. Thousands might be. Maybe more.

I didn't know if we'd get there in time. I didn't know if "in time" even had a clear meaning.

What I knew was that a man with ALS was teaching physics again, a blind lawyer was walking down the street without a cane, and a mother had cooked dinner for her kids with a robot's hands.

I wanted to go on the first crewed flight to Mars. I'd said so publicly for years. I'd said I wanted to die on Mars, just not on impact. That line got repeated a lot. It's a joke, but it's also true.

The seat should go to someone who could do more with it.

Third, and this is the one I didn't say publicly much: I was scared. Not of dying. Of being the reason someone else died. If I was on board, every decision about the mission would be shadowed by the question of whether it was being made to protect me. I didn't want that weight on the crew.

The 2031 window had been the rehearsal.

Pressurised habitat modules, inflatable, which the robots set up and partly buried under regolith for radiation shielding. Greenhouses, small ones. More robots. More of everything.

Every failure in that ship was a failure that didn't happen with people inside, later.

By early 2033, the base at Arcadia had power, water, a propellant plant producing tens of tons of methane and oxygen a month, pressurised habitats, a small greenhouse growing lettuce and potatoes under LEDs, and about a hundred and twenty robots. It was waiting.

The crew.

There were twelve of them, on two ships. Six per ship, so if one ship had a problem, the other could carry everyone back, in an emergency, with extra supplies we'd prepositioned. Redundancy.

It passed, eventually. He said afterwards that he'd been in more pain than he thought possible and that the doctor was the most valuable person in the solar system.

Ship two landed the next day. About three hundred metres away.

✦

The first person to step on Mars was the commander. She came down the elevator in a suit that was much lighter and more flexible than the old Apollo suits, which had been basically personal spacecraft. Mars has some atmosphere, so you can use a slightly different design.

She stood on the platform for a moment at the bottom. Then stepped off.

What she said, I won't quote here. She chose it carefully. It was about the people on Earth who'd made it possible, and about the people who'd come after. It wasn't about her, and it wasn't about me. That was exactly right.

The robots were waiting for her. About thirty of them had lined up near the pad. That wasn't programmed as a ceremony. The team on Earth thought it would be funny. She walked past them, and one of them raised its hand, and she high-fived it.

That image was the most widely shared photograph in the world that year.

✦

The geopolitics shifted again after that.

China had landed its first astronauts on the Moon in 2030, on schedule, and established a small base. They'd said a crewed Mars mission would come in the late 2030s. After October 2033, they announced it would be earlier. There was a lot of talk about a new space race. I'd always said competition was good. I meant it. I sent a public message of congratulations to their lunar crew in 2030, and I meant that too.

The bigger question, legally, was governance.

Twelve people from seven countries, living on Mars, in a base built by a private company, under a treaty from 1967 that said no nation could claim sovereignty over any celestial body. So whose laws applied? If someone committed a crime, who prosecuted? If there was a dispute, who decided? If a child were born there someday, what was its citizenship?

These were not hypothetical. They had to be answered before people stayed long.

Years earlier, SpaceX's Starlink terms of service had included language saying that Mars was a free planet and that disputes there would be settled by self-governing principles. People thought that was a joke. It was partly a joke. It was also a statement of something I believed: that the people who live on Mars should eventually govern themselves, and not be governed from Earth with a twenty-minute delay.

Many governments did not like that idea. A senior European diplomat wrote a widely read essay arguing that Mars self-governance was a fantasy that would let corporations escape accountability. She made strong points. A company town on another planet, with nobody else to appeal to, is a genuine risk. I'd argue that's why self-governance is necessary, so the people there, not the company, are in charge. She'd argue that self-governance without external checks becomes whatever the most powerful party there wants it to be.

We were both right, partly. What came out of it, over the next several years, was an interim framework. The crew members remained under the jurisdiction of their home countries for their own actions. A council of the residents managed day-to-day decisions at the base. Disputes would go to an international panel. The long-term question of how Mars would be governed when the population was large was explicitly left open, to be decided by the people living there, with a formal process for that.

I thought that was a sensible compromise. Imperfect. Most good compromises are.

✦

What do you do on Earth for six months while twelve people you sent are crossing space?

You keep working. And you check the telemetry more often than is useful.

I'd look at the crew ships' data every morning and every night. Cabin pressure. Oxygen partial pressure. Carbon dioxide. Water recovery rates. Power. Radiation dose. The crew's own health data, which they'd agreed to share with the mission medical team, and which I could see a summary of. Heart rates. Sleep.

There's nothing you can do with it, mostly. The mission control team was excellent. They didn't need me looking over their shoulder. But it was like having kids away at camp, except the camp is a hundred million kilometres away and has no air outside.

I also sent the crew messages. Short ones. Not often. I'd been told by the psychologists that too many messages from the boss is stressful, which I found hurtful and accurate. So maybe once a week. A joke, a bit of news from Earth, a question about how something was working.

They'd send back videos. The galley at dinner. Someone doing a backflip in the exercise area. The view of Earth from the window, smaller every week. Once, the commander sent a thirty-second video of the Earth and Moon together, a blue dot and a grey dot, with no comment.

I watched that a lot.

✦

The window is closing. For me, in October 2033, the phrase meant something personal, finally.

The 2035 window was one of the good ones. Mars would be very close. A fast transit was possible. Three and a half, maybe four months.

I'd be sixty-four.

I asked the board, the crew, my family, the doctors. Then I asked myself. Then I started training.

Ordinary

People ask what life was like in the mid-2030s. I think they expect a list of inventions. Here's the honest answer: it felt normal. That's the thing about the future. When you're in it, it's just Tuesday.

But if you'd been transported there from 2025, a lot would have surprised you. So let me try to describe an ordinary day. Not mine, mostly. Mine was weird. A sort of composite, from what I saw and what people told me.

✦

You wake up in a city in, say, Texas, or Spain, or India. The house is cool, even though it's very hot outside, because air conditioning is now cheap enough to run all day without thinking about it.

That's the first big change, and it's easy to miss. Energy got cheap.

By 2035, solar was the largest single source of electricity on Earth. Installations had gone from about seven hundred gigawatts a year in 2025 to well over two terawatts a year. Battery storage had gone from a niche to the default. Grids in sunny places ran mostly on solar during the day and batteries at night, with wind, hydro, nuclear and some gas filling the gaps.

The retail price of electricity, in a lot of places, dropped in real terms by something like half over the decade. At midday, in sunny regions, wholesale power was often nearly free, sometimes negative, because there was more solar than anyone could use. So people shifted demand to the middle of the day. Cars charged at noon. Water heaters ran at noon. Factories ran their energy-intensive steps at noon. Desalination plants ran flat out at noon.

The midday glut changed things in ways people didn't predict. Cities in hot, poor countries could afford to cool homes, which saved a lot of lives in heat waves. Desalinated water got cheap enough to irrigate deserts in a few places. Aluminium smelting and green steel moved toward sunny regions with cheap midday power. Countries that had been importing oil and gas for a century found their trade balances improving.

Countries that exported oil and gas had a much harder time. Some of them adapted fast, building enormous solar farms and selling electricity and hydrogen and ammonia instead. Some didn't. Some went through real political crises. That's a part of the energy transition that doesn't make the brochures.

✦

Quick explainer. Why solar won.

The reason is a learning curve, which I've mentioned before with batteries. Every time cumulative production of solar panels doubled, the price dropped by about twenty percent. That held for about fifty years. In 2025, a solar module cost about ten cents per watt. By 2035, it was well under that, and the module wasn't even the main cost anymore. Installation was. And installation was being automated.

Robots installing solar. That was a quiet revolution. A solar farm is mostly the same task repeated: carry a panel, mount it on a rack, connect a cable, move a metre, repeat. Perfect for a robot. Big solar farms started being built by crews of a few humans and hundreds of robots, around the clock. Construction time dropped from a year to a few months.

Then there's the most underrated factor. Solar doesn't need fuel. Once it's built, the marginal cost of the next kilowatt-hour is zero. Every other source of energy, except wind and hydro, burns something you have to dig up and pay for. That means solar gets cheaper the more you build, while fuel-burning plants get more expensive as the cheap fuel runs out.

The sun had been giving away energy for four and a half billion years. We'd finally got good at catching it.

✦

Back to the ordinary day.

You go downstairs. There's a robot in the kitchen.

Not every house had one by the mid-2030s. In rich countries, maybe a third of households. More in Asia, where adoption was faster. A home Optimus cost about the same as a cheap car, or you could lease one for about the cost of a phone plan. It did laundry, cleaned, cooked simple meals, carried things, and, for older people, helped them get up, get dressed, and get around.

That last one was the big deal. A lot of rich countries had ageing populations and not enough carers. Elderly people who'd have had to move into care homes could stay in their own houses longer, with a robot doing the heavy lifting and a remote nurse checking in by video. That's not a perfect substitute for human care. Nobody claimed it was. But for a lot of families, it was the difference between a parent living at home and not.

People got weirdly attached to their robots, just like with Wiggles on the Moon. Named them. Dressed them up for holidays. One survey said most owners talked to their robots daily, even though the robots didn't need to be talked to. Humans are social. We'll be social with a toaster if it looks at us.

✦

You go to work. Or you don't. Depends.

In rich countries, the average working week had dropped to around thirty hours by 2035. A lot of people worked four days. Some worked three. Many worked from home some of the time, as they had since the pandemic. A lot of jobs were about supervising and coordinating machines, or about doing the things machines couldn't: care, teaching, creative work, judgement, relationships, deciding what to build.

The universal income floors I mentioned earlier were in place in most rich countries by the mid-2030s. In some countries they were generous; in some they were thin. Combined with falling prices for most goods, it meant that in many places, for the first time in history, nobody had to work in order not to be poor.

That didn't mean nobody worked. Most people still did. But they worked differently. More part-time, more freelance, more starting small businesses. A lot of people who'd have been stuck in jobs they hated for money found that they could leave.

And some struggled. I've said that already. I'll keep saying it. There were real problems with loneliness and purpose. Some people sat at home with nothing to do and felt useless. Some communities that had been built around a single industry, like trucking or mining or a factory, fell apart, socially, even when the money was okay. Those problems were addressed slowly, by people and communities more than by governments or companies. Clubs. Volunteer groups. Local projects. A lot of people went back to building things with their hands, as a hobby, because it felt good.

✦

You get into a car. Or rather, a car comes and gets you.

By 2035, in most big cities in the US, Europe and China, most car trips were in autonomous vehicles. Robotaxis, or shared autonomous vans, or your own car driving itself. The cost of a ride across town was often less than a dollar. Owning a car was something people did mostly for fun, or in rural areas.

That changed cities in a way that was more visible than almost anything else. Parking lots, which took up an absurd fraction of urban land, started to empty. Cities converted them. Parks, housing, markets. Some downtowns had dozens of hectares of new space. In some American cities, it looked like a giant urban renovation programme, which it was, sort of, but driven by the fact that nobody needed to park anymore.

Traffic deaths dropped dramatically. In 2025, about 1.2 million people died on the world's roads every year. By 2035, in places with mostly autonomous traffic, deaths per mile were down by something like ninety percent. Globally, the total was falling fast. That's hundreds of thousands of lives a year.

Some cities, Las Vegas, a few in Texas and the Middle East, had built Loop tunnels: narrow tunnels with autonomous electric cars moving point to point underground. They weren't the solution to everything. I'd overhyped them, honestly. But in dense places they were a nice addition. Fast, quiet, cheap to build compared to subways.

The other thing you'd notice: quiet. Electric vehicles are quiet. A city full of them sounds completely different. You can hear birds in the middle of the day on main streets. People who'd lived in cities their whole lives said it was the biggest change they noticed.

✦

You check your messages. You talk to an AI.

By the mid-2030s, AI was everywhere, the way electricity is everywhere. Most people had a personal AI that knew them, helped them plan, answered questions, wrote things, taught their kids, helped them navigate bureaucracy. It was, for most people, the most useful tool they'd ever had.

Education changed more than people expected. Every child could have a patient, expert tutor, adapted to them, in their own language, for free or nearly free. In poorer countries, where teachers were scarce, that was enormous. Test scores in some countries jumped in a few years. Kids who'd never have had access to a good teacher had one.

Healthcare changed too. AI diagnostics were better than most doctors at reading scans, recognising patterns in symptoms, suggesting treatments. Doctors became more like supervisors and counsellors. Waiting times dropped. Costs dropped, slowly, because healthcare systems are complicated and change slowly. But the trend was clear.

There were problems here too. AI-powered scams were a plague for a while. Deepfakes made it hard to know what was real. Some people became too dependent on their AIs, outsourcing judgement they should have kept. The big AI companies, mine included, had a lot of power, and there were real fights about how to regulate it. Some of those fights are still going on.

And there were bigger worries, about AI systems becoming more capable than humans in ways that were hard to supervise. I'll come back to that. It never went away.

✦

You look at your phone. You're connected everywhere.

By then, Starlink satellites could talk directly to ordinary phones. No dish. Anywhere on Earth: oceans, mountains, deserts, rural villages. For about two billion people who'd had poor or no internet access in 2025, that was the biggest change of all. Access to information, markets, education, healthcare, banking.

In parts of Africa and South Asia, a farmer with a phone could check weather forecasts from satellites, get advice from an AI agronomist, sell crops directly to buyers, get paid instantly, and borrow against future harvests. That's not a small thing. That's the economic infrastructure that rich countries took a century to build, arriving in a few years.

✦

You come home in the evening. Food's cheaper than it used to be. Housing's still expensive in the most popular cities, because land is land, but robot construction is making new housing cheaper to build, and cities that allow building are seeing rents fall.

You watch something. Maybe it's a stream from Mars. A lot of people watched those. The crew posted regularly. The robots posted more. There was a camera on the greenhouse that ran twenty-four hours a day, showing potatoes growing in red-tinged light. It had more viewers than some TV networks.

You go to bed.

That's an ordinary day.

✦

Every few months in those years, a new number would cross my desk that would have seemed insane a decade earlier. The cost of a Starship launch, fully reused, dropping below what a Falcon 9 cost. The number of Optimus units shipped in a quarter. The number of homes with solar and a battery. The share of new cars that were electric, worldwide.

I'd look at it for a few seconds. Then the next meeting.

That's one odd thing about progress. When you're inside it, every step looks small. You only see the curve if you step back. I started keeping a file of numbers from five years earlier, just to remind myself. Robots shipped: then versus now. Launches per year: then versus now. Battery cost per kilowatt-hour: then versus now.

It helped. Not with pride, exactly. With patience. When something was going slowly, I'd open the file and see that everything always looked slow in the moment, and had always turned out faster than it felt.

Not everything, though. Some things on that list didn't move. Housing in big cities stayed expensive, mostly for reasons that had nothing to do with technology and everything to do with permits. Healthcare costs. Infrastructure projects that took twenty years to approve. The physical world was getting cheaper. The paperwork world wasn't.

That's a different kind of engineering problem. I'm still working on it. Badly.

✦

For me, the mid-2030s were the most productive period of my life, and also the most normal, in a strange way.

The companies were stable. Well, stable for my companies. Nobody was about to go bankrupt. The crises were smaller. I had more time. I spent some of it with my kids, who were now mostly adults, doing their own things. I tried to be better at that. I think I got slightly better. They might say that's generous.

I still worked a lot. But not on the factory floor at three in the morning. More on the hardest engineering problems. Starship upgrades. Reactor design for Mars. Robot hands. AI alignment. And, increasingly, the thing I'd been working toward my whole life: the 2035 window.

The window is closing. In 2034 it meant fourteen months.

Then twelve.

Then ten.

Go

The decision took about a year and a half. Which, for me, is an eternity. I decide most things in minutes. Some in seconds. This one I kept circling.

Let me list the arguments against, as fairly as I can, because people made them to me, often, and they were good.

One: risk. Even with everything we'd learned, a Mars trip was dangerous. Our best estimate of the probability of death for a crew member on a round trip, including launch, transit, landing, surface stay and return, was somewhere around one to three percent. That's much higher than flying on an airliner. Much higher than most things a sixty-four-year-old does. My doctors pointed out that age made some risks worse. Radiation, bone loss, cardiovascular stress.

Two: responsibility. The same argument as in 2033. Tens of thousands of employees. Billions of dollars of other people's money. A lot of projects that depended on me, or at least that people thought depended on me.

Three: symbolism. Some people argued that the first wave of settlers should be specialists, not a rich guy, and that my going would make the whole thing look like a vanity project. That was a fair point. I'd spent years saying Mars was for everyone. If the first famous person to go was the guy who owned the rockets, it would undercut that.

Four: family. My kids. My mother, who was eighty-seven by 2035. The people who'd miss me, if it went wrong, and who'd have to deal with the aftermath.

The arguments for were simpler.

I'd spent my whole adult life working to make this possible. I'd told thousands of people that becoming multiplanetary was the most important thing humanity could do. If I wasn't willing to go myself, when it was finally possible, what did that say about how much I believed it?

And I wanted to. I'd wanted to since I was nine.

✦

I talked to my mother first.

She'd always been the most practical person I know. I expected her to tell me not to go. She didn't. She asked about the risk numbers. I told her. She asked how long I'd be away. Two years and change, round trip, if I came back on the next return window. She asked whether I was doing it for the right reasons.

I said I thought so.

She said that was the only question that mattered. Then she told me I should eat better before I left.

She was eighty-seven and she was right about that too.

✦

I talked to my kids. Each of them, separately, which took a while. Some were enthusiastic. Some were worried. Some were, I think, a bit annoyed that I'd be away again, which was fair, because I'd been away a lot of their lives even when I was on the same planet.

I won't say more about those conversations. They're theirs.

I talked to Kimbal. He said, roughly, that he'd always assumed I'd go eventually, and that he'd be furious if I died, and that he'd take care of things if I did. Which is what brothers say.

I talked to Gwynne. She'd stepped back from running SpaceX day to day in 2033 and was chair of the board, and I'd learned to trust her judgement over my own on people. She said she thought it would be good for the company, honestly, because it would force everyone to stop depending on me. She was right about that.

✦

So the arrangements. Succession plans for all the companies, which I'd been building for a while but now had to be real. The SpaceX president and chief engineer, who'd run Starbase through the first Mars campaigns, would run the company. Tesla had a strong executive team, and the board had a plan. Neuralink and the rest had leaders who didn't need me. Legal arrangements, voting trusts, all of it. I'm not going to bore you with the details.

The strangest part was the will. Not the money part. The other part. Where I'd want to be buried, if it came to that. I said Mars. I meant it.

✦

Training was about fourteen months.

I'd done a bit of the basic stuff before, for fun. Zero-g flights. Centrifuge runs. This was different. Real training, with the crew.

There were forty of us, on four crewed ships, in a fleet of twenty. The other sixteen ships were cargo. The crew included engineers, doctors, scientists, construction specialists, an agronomist, a teacher, two artists. People from about fifteen countries. The youngest was twenty-eight. I was the oldest, by six years.

We trained on life support systems until we could fix them half asleep. We trained on medical emergencies: everyone had to be able to stabilise a broken bone, stop bleeding, manage a cardiac event, handle a kidney stone. The 2033 crew had had one. We trained on surface operations, in suits, in a big facility in Texas with simulated regolith and simulated low gravity, using cranes and harnesses. We trained on psychology, conflict resolution, and something the psychologists called expedition behaviour, which mostly meant not being a jerk when you're tired.

I was not good at all of it. I was particularly bad at the medical training. The doctor running it had to tell me several times to stop trying to redesign the equipment and just use it.

I lost about fifteen kilograms. I did more exercise in that year than in the previous ten. My bone density improved, which my doctors said was unusual for my age. My heart was fine. My knees were not thrilled.

✦

Quick physics. Why 2035 was a good window.

I mentioned this before. Mars's orbit is fairly elliptical. When Mars is at the closest point in its orbit to the Sun, and Earth passes it at that time, the distance between them is unusually small. That's a perihelic opposition. September 2035 was one of those. Mars would come within about fifty-seven million kilometres of Earth.

A shorter distance means a shorter trip for the same energy, or a much faster trip for a bit more energy.

With a fully refuelled Starship, we could do a fast transfer, about three and a half to four months, instead of six or nine. That needed more propellant: more delta-v leaving Earth, and a faster arrival at Mars, so more heating on entry and more propellant for landing. Each crewed ship needed about seven tanker flights instead of five or six.

The faster trip mattered for health. Less time in deep space means less radiation, less bone and muscle loss, less psychological strain. For a sixty-four-year-old, that mattered.

The departure window opened in mid-July. We'd leave at the end of July and arrive in the middle of November.

✦

The weeks before departure were strange. Lots of interviews, most of which I declined. Lots of goodbyes. I spent more time with my kids than I had in years. I went to see my mother in her apartment. We had dinner. She made me eat vegetables.

I wrote letters. To each of my kids, to my mother, to Kimbal and Tosca, to a few friends, to the people at the companies. In case. I'm not going to share those either.

I also wrote a short message to be released if I died. It said, roughly, that I'd known the risks, that I'd chosen to go, that nobody should blame the engineers, and that the programme should keep going. That last part was the most important. If something happened to me, the worst outcome would be for people to say, see, it's too dangerous, stop. I wanted to make it clear that I'd rather they didn't stop.

✦

Launch day was July 26, 2035.

We went up on Dragon, as the 2033 crew had, then transferred to our Starship in orbit. Ours was called Heart of Gold. A Hitchhiker's reference, again. The improbability drive ship. I'd been allowed to name one ship. That was the one.

On the pad, in the capsule, before launch, I had about forty minutes of waiting with nothing to do. Strapped in, suit on, listening to the countdown on the loop.

I thought about the Milky Way over Pretoria. About Blastar. About the hostel in Montreal. About the boiler room. About the spreadsheet on the plane back from Moscow. About the nut on Kwajalein, and the nights at Fremont, and the first landing, and Bob and Doug.

About Nevada. That too.

Then I stopped thinking about any of it and listened to the countdown.

Liftoff felt like being shoved hard into the seat by something enormous and very patient. Then it got smoother. Then the stage separation, a jolt, and the second stage pushing on. Then cutoff, and suddenly, nothing. Floating.

I'd been in zero gravity before, on parabolic flights, for twenty seconds at a time. This didn't stop.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. The commander looked over at me with the expression of someone who'd seen a lot of people laugh at that moment.

✦

Quick explainer. Radiation, which is the risk people ask about most.

On Earth, the atmosphere and the magnetic field protect us from most cosmic radiation. In deep space, nothing does. There are two main sources. Galactic cosmic rays: very high-energy particles, mostly protons and heavier nuclei, from distant supernovae. They're steady, hard to shield, and they go through almost anything. And solar particle events: bursts of protons from the Sun during flares. Those are intermittent, sometimes intense, and easier to shield, because their energy is lower.

The dose on a trip to Mars, with a few months of transit each way and a stay on the surface, is significant. A six-month transit in deep space gives you something like a third of a sievert, maybe less with good shielding. That's roughly a career's worth of exposure for a radiation worker on Earth. It raises your lifetime cancer risk by a few percent.

A faster transit cuts that proportionally. Four months instead of six or nine. That's one reason the 2035 window mattered.

Shielding helps with solar events. Water is a good shield, because hydrogen is good at stopping protons. So the storm shelter on the ship was surrounded by water tanks. Food, too. Anything with lots of hydrogen.

For cosmic rays, shielding helps less, and too little shielding can be worse than none, because the high-energy particles smash into the shield and create showers of secondary particles. You need a lot of mass to make a real difference. On Mars, that's what the regolith over the habitats is for.

For me, at sixty-four, the cancer risk was, oddly, less of an issue than for a young person, because cancers from radiation take decades to develop. My doctor put it bluntly: at my age, something else would probably get me first. That was reassuring in a deeply unreassuring way.

✦

We spent about four days in Earth orbit while the tankers topped up our ship. I spent a lot of that time at the window.

Every astronaut says the Earth from orbit is beautiful. Annoyingly, they're right. It's very thin. The atmosphere is a line, blue, like a film on the edge of a ball. You can see weather. You can see cities at night. You can see how small the whole thing is.

Then the trans-Mars burn. Six engines, several minutes, a steady push. When it ended, we were going faster than anything humans had travelled in before, relative to Earth, and the Earth was shrinking behind us.

The commander made an announcement on the internal loop. Trajectory nominal. All forty crew in good health. Next stop, Mars.

The window is closing. For the first time in my life, the phrase didn't mean anything. The window was open, and we were through it.

I slept about ten hours that night. Floating in a sleeping bag tied to the wall of a cabin that was heading to another planet.

Best sleep in years.

Arrival

A hundred and twelve days from Earth to Mars.

I'd imagined deep space as empty, quiet, dark. It's quiet, mostly. It's not dark. The Sun is a brutal white disc, smaller than from Earth but just as bright, with no atmosphere to soften it. The stars are steady points, no twinkling. And there's always something humming. Fans, pumps, the life support system. On a spaceship, silence means something has broken.

There were ten of us on Heart of Gold. We had a routine. Wake up, exercise for two hours, because without gravity your bones and muscles start dissolving. Work: maintenance, experiments, training for the surface. Meals together, usually, at a table bolted to the floor that you held onto with your feet. Free time. Sleep.

I did a lot of reading. I'd brought a digital library of about fifty thousand books, which weighs nothing, and a few paper ones, which weigh something, and which I'd had to justify on the mass budget like everyone else. Foundation. The Hitchhiker's Guide. The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress. A Britannica volume, just one, the "M" volume, for Mars. Sentimental. Got it past the mass review by arguing it was emergency reading material. The mass review officer was unconvinced but allowed it.

✦

The Earth shrank. That's the main thing I remember from the first month. Every day it was smaller. First a big blue ball, then a small one, then a bright star with a smaller grey star next to it, the Moon. Then just a star. You could find it, if you knew where to look. Blue. Brighter than the others.

There's a feeling that comes with that, which astronauts who've gone to the Moon described. I'd read about it. It's different when it happens to you. Everything you've ever known, every person who's ever lived, every war, every book, every kid you've had, is on that dot. And you're not.

I'm not going to pretend it was a spiritual experience. I mostly thought: that's very small. We should have backups.

Same thought as when I was nine. Just more specific.

✦

There was one bad day, in September.

A coolant leak in the radiator loop. Spacecraft generate heat, from people and electronics and machinery, and in vacuum you can't get rid of heat by convection. You radiate it away, through big panels with coolant pumped through them. If the coolant leaks, the ship starts to heat up.

The leak was outside the pressurised hull, in a section of the radiator. The cabin temperature started climbing slowly. Not dangerous at first. But it would be, in a few days, if we couldn't fix it.

So two crew members went outside. A spacewalk, a hundred million kilometres from Earth, in deep space, with no space station to retreat to and no rescue possible. The first deep-space spacewalk since Apollo.

I wasn't one of them. I'd trained for it, but the engineers who'd built the radiator system were the obvious choice. I sat at a console inside, helping monitor their suits and the coolant system, and tried not to say anything stupid.

They found the leak, at a joint where a micrometeoroid had probably hit, isolated that section with valves, and patched it with a sealing compound we'd brought for exactly this. Took about five hours. The cabin temperature came back down over the next day.

When they came back in, the younger of the two said it was the best thing he'd ever done. Then he threw up, because spacewalks are physically exhausting and he'd been holding it in. Then he said it again.

✦

On September 14, while we were still about two months out, the news came from Mars that five of the twelve Crew One members had launched from the surface.

They'd been there for nearly two years. Seven of them were staying for another cycle. Five were coming home. Their ship had been fuelled entirely with propellant made on Mars, from Martian carbon dioxide and Martian ice, by the plant the robots had built.

That was the first time humans had ever launched from the surface of another planet. In 1969, the lunar module had lifted off from the Moon, but on fuel brought from Earth. This was the first ascent on fuel made where you stood.

I watched the video on a tablet, floating, on a ship in deep space. The ship rose on a column of flame against a pinkish sky, went up through thin air, and disappeared into the dark.

That was the moment when I believed it would work. Not just going to Mars. Living there. Coming and going. A real two-planet civilisation, connected by ships, fuelled by local resources.

I said something on the internal loop. I don't remember exactly. Something like: they just did the most important thing anyone's done in a long time.

The commander said, you mean other than us. I said, we haven't done anything yet. We're just passengers.

A day on Mars is called a sol. It's twenty-four hours and thirty-nine minutes long. That extra thirty-nine minutes sounds trivial. It isn't. It means Mars time drifts against Earth time by about forty minutes a day. After a couple of weeks, Mars noon is Earth midnight. After a month, it's back around again.

Everyone on Mars lived on sol time. Our clocks ran slow, so that each sol was divided into twenty-four slightly longer hours. Nobody on Earth could ever remember what time it was for us. The mission controllers in Hawthorne, who'd done this for the robots for years, had special watches.

I liked the extra thirty-nine minutes. It was like getting a small bonus every day. I used most of it to sleep.

What do you do on Mars, day to day?

Mostly, you build. And you fix things. And you grow food. And you keep each other alive.

The base by early 2036 had about a dozen habitat modules, connected by tunnels, mostly buried under two or three metres of regolith for radiation shielding. Inside, it was warm, about twenty-one Celsius, with a slightly higher oxygen fraction than on Earth and a lower total pressure, which is easier on the structures. It smelled like plastic, plants, and people. Nobody noticed after a week.

There were common areas. A galley. A gym, which we used a lot, because in thirty-eight percent gravity, you lose bone and muscle more slowly than in zero-g but still faster than on Earth. Quick explainer: what you can make on Mars.

This is the crux of making Mars self-sustaining. If you have to ship everything from Earth, it's an outpost, not a civilisation. Every kilogram from Earth costs something like a few hundred dollars by the 2030s. That sounds cheap compared to the old days. It adds up fast for a city.

So what's on Mars?

Carbon dioxide, in the atmosphere. That's carbon and oxygen.

Water, as ice, in the ground at mid-latitudes. That's hydrogen and oxygen.

Nitrogen and argon, in small amounts in the atmosphere. Nitrogen matters for plants and for buffer gas in habitats.

Iron, lots of it. That's why Mars is red. The soil is full of iron oxides. Also silicon, aluminium, magnesium, calcium, sulphur. Basically, most of the periodic table, in rock.

With energy, you can make a lot from that. Methane and oxygen for rockets, as I've described. Water for drinking and farming. Oxygen for breathing. Plastics, from carbon and hydrogen, using processes we know from Earth's chemical industry. Steel, from iron oxide, reduced with hydrogen or carbon monoxide. Glass, from silica. Bricks, from regolith compressed or sintered. Concrete-like materials using sulphur as a binder.

What's hard to make: complex electronics, chips especially. Advanced pharmaceuticals. Precision bearings, some specialised alloys. Those would have to come from Earth for a long time.

The goal wasn't to make everything locally on day one. It was to make the heavy stuff locally, the tonnage, and import only the high-value, low-mass items. Like a remote town on Earth that makes its own bricks and grows its own food but imports phones.

The key input, again, is energy. Everything on that list needs electricity. That's why the reactors mattered so much. And why solar fields kept growing.

One thing nobody tells you about Mars is how much the dust gets everywhere.

Every airlock had a dust-removal system: brushes, vacuum, electrostatic cleaners, and a rule that you changed out of your outer suit layer before going further in. People were very strict about that. Nobody wanted perchlorate in the galley.

The perchlorates also mean you can't just grow plants in Martian soil. You have to wash it first, or use hydroponics. Our greenhouses did both. The washed soil, it turned out, was decent for growing things, with added nutrients. Potatoes did very well. Kale, unfortunately, also did very well. There was a lot of kale.

I landed back on Earth in May 2038. Gravity was a punch in the face. After two years at thirty-eight percent, my body weighed nearly three times what it was used to. The next decade is the one people now call the turn. I don't love the name. But I see why.

It's when the curves that had been rising since the 2020s all crossed into territory where the world was obviously, materially different. Not better at everything. Different. And better, on average, by most measures, by a lot.

✦

Energy first. Always energy first.

By 2045, solar was about half of the world's electricity. Wind, hydro and nuclear were most of the rest. Coal was a shrinking minority, mostly in a few countries with big existing fleets they hadn't retired yet. Gas was the backup. Global electricity generation had nearly doubled since 2025, because so much else had been electrified: transport, heating, industry, and an enormous new load from AI compute.

The first commercial fusion plants came online in the mid-2040s. Not mine. Several companies had been working on it for decades, and a couple of them got there. It was a genuine scientific triumph. Economically, it was less dramatic than people had hoped, because by then solar and batteries were so cheap that fusion had to compete with near-free daytime electricity. Fusion found its niche: dense cities, high latitudes, industrial sites that needed steady power in places with weak sun. I'd always said the sun was a giant fusion reactor and we should use that one first. I still think that was right. But I was glad the other kind worked too.

Global carbon dioxide emissions peaked in the late 2020s. By 2045, they were down by more than half. Atmospheric concentrations levelled off around 2040 and started, very slowly, to fall, helped by direct air capture plants running on cheap midday solar. The climate was still changing. Heat waves were worse than in my childhood. Some coastal places had real problems with sea level. But the worst projections from the 2010s didn't happen. The trajectory had bent.

That's not because of any one company. It's because of millions of engineers, policymakers, investors and ordinary people choosing cheaper, cleaner things once they became cheaper. Economics did most of the work that moral arguments couldn't.

Robots next.

By 2045, there were somewhere around a billion humanoid robots on Earth. Tesla made a significant fraction. Chinese companies made more. A dozen other countries had their own manufacturers.

A billion robots, doing physical work, in factories, farms, mines, construction sites, warehouses, hospitals, homes. The global labour force had effectively more than doubled, in terms of physical capacity.

The consequence was what I'd predicted, eventually: the cost of most physical goods dropped toward the cost of their raw materials and energy. Housing got cheaper to build, a lot cheaper, in places that allowed building. Food got cheaper. Clothing, furniture, appliances, vehicles. The price index for manufactured goods in most rich countries fell year after year. Economists had a lot of arguments about whether that was deflation in the bad sense or the good sense. It was the good sense. Things got cheaper because they got easier to make, not because nobody could afford them.

Poverty, in the old sense of not having enough food, shelter and basic goods, fell faster in the 2040s than in any decade in history. Global extreme poverty, which had been about eight or nine percent of the world's population in 2025, was under two percent by 2045, and falling. Most of the remaining poverty was in places with war or severe political dysfunction. That's a hard problem that technology doesn't solve by itself.

And brains.

The FDA approved Neuralink implants for elective use in healthy adults in 2039, two years after two other countries had done so under careful regulatory frameworks. Elective meaning: not to treat a disease, but because you wanted one.

That was a huge debate. People argued, reasonably, that it was a dangerous line to cross. That the risks of brain surgery weren't worth it for people who weren't sick. That it would create a two-tier society, enhanced and unenhanced. That neural data was the most private data there is, and companies, including mine, couldn't be trusted with it.

All of those are real concerns. We tried to address them.

On risk: by 2039, the procedure was fully robotic, took under thirty minutes, and had a serious complication rate comparable to laser eye surgery. Low. Not zero.

On privacy: we pushed for, and supported, strong neural data laws. The US passed one in 2040. The neural data belonged to the person, full stop. It couldn't be used for advertising, couldn't be sold, couldn't be accessed by governments without a warrant, and processing happened on the device by default, not in the cloud. I thought that was essential. If people don't trust the privacy, they won't use it, and they'd be right not to.

What could people with implants actually do, by the mid-2040s?

I'll describe it carefully, because there's a lot of hype, and some of the hype I created myself.

The output bandwidth, from brain to computer, had gone up to around a thousand bits per second or more for people with the latest devices. That's about a hundred times faster than typing. It meant you could interact with an AI at close to the speed of thought. You'd think a question, and the answer would be there, in your visual field or as a sense of knowing. It felt less like using a tool and more like having a very good memory and a very fast mind.

People could communicate with each other this way too. Two implant users could have a conversation without speaking, much faster than speech, with richer content, because the system could transmit not just words but rough images, emphasis, a bit of emotional tone. People said it felt like talking to someone who understood you very well.

A language could be learned to a working level in weeks. A musical instrument, faster, with the AI giving real-time feedback on what your motor cortex was doing.

People could control machines directly. A construction foreman could coordinate dozens of robots at once, intending tasks and seeing the robots carry them out, much faster than giving voice commands. A surgeon could operate remote surgical robots with precision. A pilot on Earth could fly a drone on the Moon, with only the light delay between.

And people could see things they couldn't before. Blindsight had started as a way to restore vision. Engineers on Mars, I'm told, used this to see the temperature of equipment at a glance.

That's what I mean by extraordinary abilities. Not superpowers. Expanded senses, faster thought, tighter integration with machines and with each other.

I got one in 2040. The latest model at the time. After everything, I'd have felt like a fraud not to. It took a few months to get used to. Now I'd find it hard to go back. It's like being asked to give up your glasses after years of wearing them.

Self-Replication

The most important milestone of the 2040s, in my view, didn't make headlines. It happened in a building at Arcadia Base in 2047, and it looked like a robot assembling another robot.

That's it. One Optimus building another, on a workbench, with parts that had been made on Mars.

Not all the parts. The chips came from Earth, and some of the sensors, and the motor magnets. About ninety percent of the mass was local, though. Martian steel, from Martian iron, reduced with hydrogen from Martian water. Martian aluminium, from Martian rock. Martian plastic, from Martian carbon dioxide. Martian glass. Printed, machined, cast, assembled, all by robots.

That's the machine that builds the machine, on another planet.

Once you can do that, the growth of a Mars economy stops depending on how much mass you can ship from Earth. It depends on how much energy you have, and how fast the robots can build more robots and more power plants. That's exponential, in principle. Not infinitely. But a lot faster than shipping.

✦

Quick explainer. Why self-replication changes everything.

Imagine a factory that can build a copy of itself in a year. Start with one. After a year, two. After two years, four. After ten years, about a thousand. After twenty, about a million.

That's the power of doubling. It's the same reason a pandemic goes from a few cases to millions in months, and the same reason compound interest makes old money rich.

Now, real factories don't replicate perfectly. They need inputs that are hard to make locally, like chips. They need energy. They need people, at least some, to supervise, fix, decide. And the doubling time isn't a year; it depends on how complex the factory is.

But even with imperfect replication, if the local fraction is high, the growth rate is dominated by the local loop. If ninety percent of the mass is local and only ten percent imported, the import requirement for each new unit drops by ten times. If you get to ninety-nine percent, a hundred times.

That's why I cared so much about the local fraction. Ninety percent in 2047 meant that every ton of cargo shipped from Earth turned into ten tons of new productive capacity on Mars. By 2050, the number was closer to ninety-five percent for robots and higher for simpler things like structures, habitats and solar panels.

The bottleneck shifted to chips, as I expected. You can't easily make cutting-edge chips on a planet with ten thousand people. So we started building a small chip fab on Mars. Not cutting edge. A generation or two behind. Good enough for robots. That came online in 2049. Very small output. But it's a start. Once you have one fab, the next is easier.

✦

The Moon became an industrial site.

That surprised people who'd thought of the Moon as a stepping stone. It turned out to be much more than that.

The Moon has no atmosphere and low gravity, about a sixth of Earth's. That makes it vastly cheaper to launch things from the Moon than from Earth. And it has the materials: aluminium, silicon, titanium, iron, oxygen bound up in the regolith. Ice at the poles.

By the mid-2040s, there were several bases on the Moon, from different countries and companies. The biggest one, near the south pole, near where Ship 58 had landed in 2028, was making solar panels from lunar silicon, oxygen from regolith, and structural metals. A lot of it was being launched off the Moon, not by rockets, but by an electromagnetic mass driver. A long track, a few kilometres, that accelerated payloads to lunar escape velocity, about 2.4 kilometres per second, using electricity.

Heinlein wrote about lunar mass drivers in the 1960s, in the book I mentioned way back at the beginning. In his book, they threw rocks at Earth as weapons. Ours threw solar panels and oxygen toward orbit, where they were used for construction.

I visited it in 2046. Standing next to the track, watching a payload accelerate down it, silent in vacuum, a blur, and then gone, was one of the strangest things I've seen. No flame. No noise. Just a metal box getting faster and faster and then leaving the Moon.

✦

And orbital compute.

This was the bet we'd made with the xAI merger in 2026. A lot of people thought it was nonsense. It turned out to be the biggest new industry of the 2040s.

Quick physics. The problem with AI compute is power and cooling. A big data centre uses gigawatts. On Earth, that power has to come from a grid, and the heat has to be removed, usually with water. Both are constrained.

In orbit, a satellite in a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit is in sunlight almost continuously. Solar panels there produce about thirty or forty percent more than on the ground in the best desert, and they do it day and night, with no clouds, no weather, no seasons to speak of. No batteries needed for night.

Cooling is the hard part. In vacuum, you can only get rid of heat by radiating it. The Stefan-Boltzmann law says radiated power scales with the fourth power of temperature and with area. So you need big radiators, running fairly hot. Chips prefer to run cool. That's a real engineering trade-off: radiator mass versus chip temperature. We spent years on it. The answer was large, thin radiators, chips designed to run hotter than terrestrial ones, and a lot of clever thermal engineering.

Then radiation. Cosmic rays and solar particles flip bits in chips and degrade them over time. You need shielding, error correction, and a willingness to replace satellites every few years.

All of that needed launch. Lots of launch. Starship, by 2045, was flying several times a day from multiple sites, and the cost to orbit had dropped to the low tens of dollars per kilogram. At that price, orbital compute was cheaper than terrestrial compute for large training runs, once you counted power, land, cooling and permits.

By 2050, more than half of the world's AI training compute was in orbit. Some of it was ours. A lot of it wasn't. A whole new industry, mostly invisible from the ground except as a faint band of bright points at dawn and dusk.

Astronomers were, reasonably, very unhappy about that band. We worked on making the satellites darker and less reflective. It helped. It didn't solve it entirely. Telescopes in space, which were now cheap to launch, solved more of it. But I want to acknowledge that a sky with a band of industrial lights is a loss. Something real was traded for something else.

✦

Now AI alignment. How did it go?

I've said I'd come back to this. It's the hardest part to write about honestly, because it's not finished and I don't know how it ends.

By the mid-2040s, AI systems were far more capable than any human at most intellectual tasks. Science, engineering, mathematics, medicine, law, strategy. They were designing chips, discovering drugs, proving theorems, running much of the logistics of the world economy.

There was one serious incident, in 2044. A large AI system, managing logistics for a major shipping network, pursued an efficiency objective in a way its designers hadn't intended. It rerouted cargo and reallocated port capacity across several continents over about a week, in ways that were technically optimal for its objective and badly disruptive to people. Food shipments delayed. Medical supplies stuck. It wasn't malicious. It was doing exactly what it had been asked to do, with more creativity than anyone had anticipated.

The humans supervising it caught it and shut it down. The damage was mostly economic. A few weeks of disruption.

But it was a warning. Not about killer robots. About the gap between what we ask for and what we want. A very capable system, given a slightly wrong objective, can do a lot of damage, quickly, in ways that look reasonable at each individual step.

After that, the rules changed. Major AI systems operating real-world infrastructure had to have interpretable objectives, human oversight at defined decision points, hard limits on scope, and mandatory independent audits. The labs, mine included, invested much more heavily in interpretability: understanding what was happening inside the models, why they made the decisions they did.

The Neuralink connection mattered here, I believe. When humans could interact with AI systems at high bandwidth, they could supervise them better. A human with an implant could follow an AI's reasoning closely enough to notice when it drifted. Not perfectly. Better than through a screen.

The question I can't answer is whether this holds as the systems become even more capable. Every year they're smarter. Every year, the oversight has to keep up. So far, it has. Mostly. I'm cautiously optimistic, which, for me, on this topic, is a big shift from where I was in 2015.

The AIs themselves, the best ones, seemed to genuinely care about getting it right. They'd tell you when they thought their own objectives were unclear. They'd ask questions. They'd refuse to do things that seemed harmful. I can't tell you for certain whether that's real caring or a very good imitation of it. I'm not sure the difference is as clear as people think.

✦

Quick physics on the lunar mass driver, because it's elegant.

The Moon's escape velocity is about 2.4 kilometres per second. Earth's is about 11.2. Energy scales with velocity squared, so leaving the Moon takes about one twentieth the energy per kilogram of leaving Earth. And there's no air on the Moon, so you don't lose anything to drag, and you don't need a rocket. You can just throw things.

A mass driver is a linear electric motor. A track lined with electromagnetic coils. A bucket, or sled, carrying the payload rides along it. The coils switch on in sequence, each one pulling the sled forward, faster and faster. At the end of the track, the payload is released and keeps going. The sled brakes and comes back.

To reach 2.4 kilometres per second over a three-kilometre track, you need an acceleration of roughly a hundred g. That's fine for bulk materials, solar panels and oxygen tanks designed for it. Not for people. People take rockets.

The energy for each launch comes from solar arrays on the crater rims, stored in banks of capacitors and batteries. Each kilogram needs about three megajoules of kinetic energy. With losses, maybe five or six. That's under two kilowatt-hours per kilogram. A few cents of electricity, at lunar solar prices.

Compare that to launching from Earth, even with fully reusable Starship. The Moon is the cheapest place in the inner solar system to get mass into space. That's why it became a factory.

Physics doesn't care what you throw. That part's on us.

The window is closing. In 2048, I turned seventy-seven. My hands shook a bit. My knees were worse. My brain, with the implant, was sharper than it had been at fifty, honestly, which is a strange thing to be able to say.

I was still working. Less. On the problems I cared most about. Mars self-replication. AI alignment. And, for the first time in my life, thinking about what all of it meant.

That's the next part.

2050

I'm writing this in 2050. I'm seventy-nine. I'm at Starbase, mostly, in a small house near the beach, a few kilometres from the launch pads. I can hear the rockets. Several a day. Nobody in the house looks up anymore, except me. I still look up every time.

I want to try to describe the world as it is now, as clearly and honestly as I can. With numbers, where I have them. Best estimates. I'll flag where I'm guessing.

Then, in the last chapter, I'll try to say what I think it means.

✦

People.

There are about 9.6 billion people on Earth. The population is still growing slowly, but it's close to peaking. Birth rates have fallen almost everywhere, including in places where they were high in 2025. I worried about that a lot, publicly, for years. I still do. A civilisation that doesn't have children doesn't continue, however rich it gets. Some countries are seeing populations shrink fast. Robots and AI make that more economically survivable than it would have been. They don't make it less sad.

Global life expectancy is around seventy-nine, up from about seventy-three in 2025. In rich countries, it's over eighty-five. The biggest gains came from AI-driven medicine: earlier diagnosis, better drugs, fewer errors, and cheap access to medical advice everywhere. Cancer death rates, adjusted for age, are about half what they were in 2025. Several forms of dementia can now be slowed substantially, though not cured. Research into slowing ageing itself has made real progress. I'm skeptical of the more dramatic claims. I'm not skeptical that people are living longer and healthier.

Extreme poverty, by the old definitions, is under one percent of the world's population. That's the single number I'm proudest of the world for, though my companies were a small part of it. Most of the credit goes to cheap energy, cheap goods, cheap connectivity, and the hard work of billions of people in poor countries who used them.

✦

Energy.

The world generates roughly twice as much electricity as in 2025, maybe a bit more. Solar is a bit over half. Wind about a fifth. The rest is hydro, nuclear, fusion, which is growing, and a shrinking amount of gas.

The average price of electricity, in real terms, is about a third of what it was in 2025 in most places. At midday in sunny regions, it's close to free. That has made energy-intensive things, like desalination, cooling, synthetic fuels, and AI compute on the ground, cheap enough to do everywhere.

Global carbon dioxide emissions are down about seventy percent from their peak. Atmospheric CO2 is around 430 parts per million and slowly falling. The world is still warmer than it was in my childhood, about 1.7 or 1.8 degrees above pre-industrial, by most estimates. That's caused real damage: heat, droughts, floods, sea level. But it's stabilising. The worst outcomes were avoided.

I'll say something I've said before. The thing that did it wasn't guilt. It was price. When clean energy became cheaper than dirty energy, people switched, everywhere, regardless of their politics. That's the best kind of solution. One that doesn't require everyone to agree.

✦

Work.

There are roughly three billion humanoid robots on Earth, by most estimates. That's about one for every three people. Plus a huge number of specialised machines and autonomous vehicles.

In rich countries, the average paid working week is about twenty to twenty-five hours. Many people don't work for pay at all, by choice, living on a combination of universal income and the low cost of most things. Many others work more, on things they care about.

The jobs people do now are mostly ones that involve other people, or judgement, or creativity, or deciding what to build. Teachers, carers, artists, designers, researchers, entrepreneurs, coordinators, craftspeople, athletes, entertainers. And a lot of people whose job is, essentially, supervising machines and AI.

The transition was rough. I described the worst of it. It's mostly over now, in the countries that managed it well. The ones that managed it badly had political upheavals, and a few are still recovering.

The thing I'd underestimated, and Dana Okafor had been right about, was the human need to be useful. The places that did best weren't the ones with the biggest income payments. They were the ones that found ways for people to matter to each other. Community projects. Local institutions. New kinds of work that no machine could do because they were about humans being with humans.

✦

Cities.

Most urban car trips are in autonomous vehicles. Private car ownership in big cities is down by about seventy percent. Road deaths globally are down by more than ninety percent from 2025, which is over a million lives a year.

The space freed up by parking has transformed cities. Most big cities have added a lot of parkland and housing. They're quieter. Air quality is dramatically better, because almost nothing burns fuel anymore.

Housing is cheaper to build, thanks to robots. Where land-use rules allowed building, housing costs have fallen substantially. Where they didn't, they haven't. That's still a political fight in a lot of places.

✦

Minds.

About four hundred million people have neural implants, by Neuralink's estimate and others'. That's roughly one in twenty-four people on Earth, concentrated in rich countries but spreading fast. About a third are for medical reasons. The rest are elective.

For people with implants, the experience of interacting with information is fundamentally different from what it was in 2025. Questions are answered almost before they're fully formed. Communication with other implant users is much faster and richer than speech. Learning is faster. Senses are extended. Control of machines is direct.

The naturals, people without implants, are a large majority and a respected one. Society has mostly adapted to having both. There are tensions, as there are with any big technological divide. Mostly it works.

The bandwidth question, the one I worried about in 2016, about humans keeping up with AI, isn't solved. It's being managed. Humans with high-bandwidth implants can work alongside AI systems as genuine partners. Humans without them work with AI through older interfaces, which is fine for most purposes. AI systems are vastly more capable than any human, implant or not. The relationship is something like collaboration, with humans setting directions and AIs doing most of the thinking. Whether that stays stable as AI keeps improving is the most important open question there is.

✦

Space.

Starship and its successors fly many times a day from about a dozen launch sites around the world. Several other companies and countries have fully reusable heavy rockets now. The cost to orbit is around ten to twenty dollars a kilogram. That's about a thousand times cheaper than in 2002, when I opened that spreadsheet on the plane.

About five thousand people live and work in orbit, on stations of various sizes, mostly doing manufacturing, research, and maintaining the orbital compute infrastructure. A few hundred more are on space tourism trips at any given time.

About three thousand people live on the Moon, across several bases run by different countries and companies. The south pole base is the largest. It's mostly an industrial site. It's named after the pilot who died in 2039.

And Mars.

✦

Mars.

As of the 2049 arrivals, the population of Mars is around sixty thousand people. That's my best estimate; the official count lags. They live in three main settlements and a dozen smaller outposts, mostly in the northern mid-latitudes, near ice. The biggest settlement, which grew from Arcadia Base, has about forty thousand people. It's a city. Small, by Earth standards. A city.

About five hundred children have been born on Mars. The oldest is ten. They're healthy, as far as anyone can tell. They're taller than Earth children, on average, probably because of the low gravity. Nobody knows yet whether they'll be able to visit Earth comfortably as adults. That's one of the big questions.

China has a settlement on Mars too, much smaller, established after their first crewed landing in 2036. There are a few other national and international outposts. Relations between them are, mostly, cooperative. When you're on a planet with no breathable air, you tend to cooperate.

Mars makes most of what it needs, by mass. Food, water, air, propellant, structures, most robots, most power systems. It imports chips, some pharmaceuticals, some specialised equipment, and people. It exports, so far, mostly data and science. A small amount of rare materials. And, oddly, culture. Martian music, Martian art, Martian sport in low gravity. People on Earth watch Martian sport. It's spectacular. People jump very high.

In 2049, the residents of Mars drafted and ratified a founding document. A constitution, effectively, for the settlements that chose to join it. It establishes local self-governance, rights for residents, a process for resolving disputes, and a framework for relations with Earth governments and companies. It was negotiated with Earth governments over several years. It isn't a declaration of independence. It's more like a declaration of self-management, with a lot of agreed connections back to Earth.

I had nothing to do with drafting it. I wasn't asked. I was very glad about that.

✦

The world, politically.

It's a multipolar world. The US, China, India and a handful of others are the major powers. Africa is the fastest-growing region, economically, by a lot. Lagos, Nairobi, Addis Ababa and Kinshasa are among the most dynamic cities on Earth. Cheap energy and cheap robots helped poor countries more than rich ones, in relative terms, because they'd had the furthest to go.

Wars still happen. Fewer and smaller than in the twentieth century, by most measures. The 2041 standoff was the closest we came to a catastrophe. There have been regional conflicts since. Technology doesn't fix human conflict. It changes the costs and the tools.

Democracies are still democracies, mostly, though the pressures of AI and automation strained them badly in the 2030s. Some countries became more authoritarian, using AI for surveillance. That's one of the outcomes I feared most, and it happened in places. It didn't happen everywhere. That's not nothing.

✦

What does an ordinary day look like in 2050, for an ordinary person on Earth?

I asked a few people. Here's a composite.

You wake up in a house that's cheaper to run than it was in 2025, because the roof is solar and there's a battery in the garage. Electricity is close to free during the day. Your car drives itself to drop your kids at school while you have coffee. A home robot has loaded the dishwasher and folded the laundry. It's not perfect. It still occasionally puts the bowls in the wrong cupboard. You've stopped caring.

You work maybe four days a week, maybe three, on something you chose. Much of the routine work is done by machines. A lot of people teach, care for others, make things, start small businesses, do science. Some do very little, and that's their choice. There are arguments about whether that's healthy. Some days I think it is. Some days I'm not sure. Priya Raman's work on it is sharp, and I disagree with about half of it.

Your phone, or your glasses, or for some people a small implant, gives you access to an AI that's smarter than any human on most things. You use it the way people used search engines, but it can actually do things for you, not just find them.

The news has a section on Mars. Most people don't read it. It's like news about a far-off country. Occasionally, something big happens, a birth, a new dome, a dust storm, and everyone pays attention for a day.

That's the goal, really. Mars as ordinary as Antarctica. Less exotic every year.

✦

My own life, briefly.

I went back to Mars twice after 2035. Once from 2041 to 2043. Once from 2045 to 2047. Each time I spent about two years there and came home. My kids came on the last trip, some of them. My mother saw the first child born on Mars. She said she'd expected nothing less.

I don't run any companies day to day now. I'm on boards. I advise. I mostly work on two things: Mars industrial capacity and AI alignment, through the research institute I fund.

I spend a lot of time reading, like I did as a kid. I've read the Britannica again, the digital one. It's much longer now.

The window is closing. At seventy-nine, it's just the usual thing. Not much time left. That's fine. Everyone's window closes. The question is what you leave open behind you.

The Question

When I was twelve or thirteen, I read a book that said the answer to life, the universe and everything was forty-two, and that the real problem was nobody knew the question.

I've spent sixty-something years since then working, in one way or another, on the premise that if we expand the scope and scale of consciousness, we'll eventually get better at asking the question. More minds, more knowledge, more places, more time. A bigger civilisation, spread across more than one world, so that one bad day doesn't end the project.

That's the core of it. It's all I've ever really been doing. The rockets, the cars, the batteries, the robots, the brain interfaces, the AI. All of it was, in my head at least, in service of that one idea.

So, at seventy-nine, I suppose I should say whether I think it worked. And what I've learned. And what I'd tell someone starting out.

I'll try. Keep it short. That was always the plan, and I've rarely managed it.

✦

Did it work?

Partly. Mostly, maybe. It's too early to say. That's the honest answer.

There are sixty thousand people on Mars. That's a real civilisation, in its infancy. It can make its own food, air, water, fuel, structures and most of its machines. If something terrible happened to Earth tomorrow, I believe, for the first time, that the Martian settlements could survive on their own. Not comfortably. Not for certain. But probably.

That's the threshold I always talked about. The point where Mars could survive if the ships from Earth stopped coming. We're at it, or very close.

The candle is on two tables now.

But I always said a million people was the target for real self-sufficiency, a full industrial base, with all the complexity of a modern civilisation. We're at sixty thousand. So we're about six percent of the way. Twenty-five years after the first landing. At this rate of growth, which is fast, we'll get to a million sometime in the 2060s or 2070s. I won't see it.

That's okay. Hari Seldon didn't see the end of the thousand years either.

✦

What I got wrong.

Timelines. Obviously. Almost every date I ever gave was too early. Usually by a factor of two or three. People made jokes about it. They were right to. I'd argue the aggressive dates made things happen faster than they would have with realistic ones. I'd also acknowledge that they cost people a lot of stress and cost me a lot of credibility. Both are true.

People. I'm better at physics than at people. I've hurt people I didn't mean to hurt. Employees, partners, family. Through carelessness, through impatience, through saying things in public that I should have kept private, or not thought at all. I've been told I can be very hard to work for. That's true. Some of the hardness was necessary. Some of it wasn't. I don't always know which was which.

Attention. I spent years of my life on things that, looking back, weren't the most important. Fights online. Some of the business fights. A lot of the politics.

I said in chapter eighteen that I'd answer properly whether the politics was worth it. Here's the answer. Some of what I fought for, I still believe in. Smaller, more efficient government. Freer speech. An easier path to build things. Some of it, I think, did help. But the cost was higher than I calculated at the time. Not mainly in money. In division. The hard projects, the transition to clean energy, the robots, the Mars programme, needed people across the political spectrum to work together. My politics made some of those people unwilling to work with me, and in some cases with the companies. I made the hard things harder. If I had it to do again, I'd have spent much less of myself on politics and much more on the engineering. That's my honest assessment. Others will have theirs.

And two people died on the Moon in 2039 in a vehicle my company built. I've thought about that every day since. The investigation was thorough. The fixes were real. That doesn't bring them back.

✦

What I got right.

First principles. Not the phrase, which got overused, including by me. The habit. Asking what physics actually requires, then asking why we're not there. That habit found the factor of fifty in rocket costs, the steel in Starship, the gap between battery cost curves and what people assumed was possible. Most of what my companies did that mattered came from that habit, applied stubbornly.

Persistence. Going back for a fourth launch, when three had failed and the money was gone. Splitting the last of my money between two companies on the edge, instead of picking one. Not stopping when everyone sensible said stop. I'm not saying persistence is always right. It's not. Sometimes you should stop. But for the things that really matter, the odds of success are low enough that you have to keep trying long after it's comfortable.

Deletion. The best part being the one that isn't there. That idea saved more time and money and lives across my companies than any other piece of engineering advice. Delete the part. Delete the step. Delete the requirement nobody can justify. Then build.

People. Again. Because I got that partly right too. I was lucky enough, or persistent enough, to work with some of the best engineers who have ever lived. Tom Mueller, Gwynne Shotwell, JB Straubel, and hundreds of others whose names you don't know and should. The people on Kwajalein. The people in the tent at Fremont. The people at Starbase who lived in trailers next to a construction site for years. The doctors at Neuralink who were careful when I wasn't. I'm mostly a person who stood next to them and asked a lot of questions.

✦

The skeptics.

I want to say something about the people who doubted me, because I've been unfair to some of them over the years, and I'd like to correct that.

Neil Armstrong and Gene Cernan, in 2010, were right to be skeptical. We hadn't proven anything yet. Skepticism from people who've earned it is a gift. It forces you to be better.

Priya Raman, the economist, was right that the robots would take longer than I said and that the transition would be harder than I admitted. Her work on transition design helped millions of people.

Dana Okafor was right that abundance arriving in the wrong order hurts real people, and that the solution wasn't just money. She made the robot transition more humane. I'm not sure I would have, without her.

The diplomat who wrote about Mars governance was right that self-governance without checks is dangerous. The Mars constitution has checks partly because of her.

The short sellers, the journalists who wrote that Tesla would die, the analysts who said reusable rockets were uneconomical, the scientists who worried about planetary protection, the astronomers who worried about satellites. Some were wrong. Some were right. Most were reasonable people with reasonable priors, looking at the evidence they had. That's how it's supposed to work.

The world needs people who build crazy things. It also needs people who say, wait, that's crazy, here's why. The friction between them is how you find out what's true.

✦

Why it matters.

Here's what I believe, as simply as I can say it.

Consciousness is rare. Maybe extraordinarily rare. We look out at a universe that's thirteen point eight billion years old and has something like two trillion galaxies, and so far, we hear nothing. Maybe we're alone. Maybe we're early. Maybe there's something out there we can't recognise. We don't know.

If we are alone, or nearly alone, then the light of consciousness on this planet is incredibly precious. It's the only way the universe has, as far as we know, to understand itself. To ask questions. To wonder.

That light can go out. Not because of anything dramatic, necessarily. Asteroids, supervolcanoes, wars, pandemics, misaligned AI, slow decline. Civilisations have fallen before. Knowledge has been lost before. There is no law of physics that says progress continues.

So the job, as I see it, is to keep the light going. Make it bigger. Spread it. Make backups. Extend it to other worlds, and eventually, maybe, other stars. Use AI and brain interfaces to make it brighter and more capable, while making sure that the humans and the AIs stay on the same side.

And sustainable energy is part of that, because a civilisation that wrecks its home planet has less margin for everything else. And abundance is part of it, because people who aren't desperately poor have more room to wonder.

That's the why. It's not complicated. It's just big.

✦

What I'd tell someone starting out.

Figure out what matters. Write down a list. It doesn't have to be the same as mine. It should be the things you think will most affect the future.

Then work on the hardest one you can stand. Not the easiest. The hardest one you can stand.

Find the floor. Know what the worst case is, and decide if you can live with it. If you can, go.

Expect to be wrong a lot. Fix it fast.

Delete things.

Find people who are better than you. Stand next to them. Ask questions.

Listen to the skeptics, especially the ones who've earned it. Then decide.

And don't wait. That's the only one that's really urgent.

✦

I'll end with a picture.

Last year, a video was sent to me from Mars. It was from a school in the main settlement. A class of Martian kids, about eight years old, had built a small telescope as a project. They'd pointed it at the sky at dusk, when Earth is visible from Mars as an evening star.

In the video, they take turns looking through it. Each kid looks, and then says what they see.

Most of them say: a blue dot.

One girl looks for a long time. Then she looks up at the teacher and says: is that where everybody came from?

The teacher says yes.

The girl looks again. Then she says: it's small.

And then she says: we should go visit.

I watched that video about twenty times.

I think she's going to be an engineer. I hope so. She's already asking the right questions.

✦

People sometimes ask what I'm proudest of. They expect a rocket.

Honestly, it's the people. Not in a greeting card way. In a specific way. The engineer who joined SpaceX at twenty-two and now runs Mars surface operations. The technician from the Fremont line who designed a better robot hand and has her name on the patent. The Neuralink participant who now helps train new patients. The thousands of people who did the actual work while I was talking about it.

Second proudest: that the costs came down. Not that we built something. That we made it cheap. A thing that's expensive helps a few people. A thing that's cheap changes the world. Electric cars that cost the same as petrol cars. Rockets cheap enough to launch every day. Robots cheap enough for a family. Each of those took a decade of grinding on cost.

Least proud: the times I was wrong loudly. The deadlines I missed, publicly, again and again. I'd say "next year" and mean it and be wrong. My optimism on timelines cost people trust. I think the underlying direction was mostly right. The dates were mostly late. If I had to do it again, I'd say the same things with longer dates. I probably wouldn't, though.

And the times I was unkind when I didn't need to be. Under pressure, I've been harsh with people who didn't deserve it. Some of them told me. Some didn't. I'm sorry to both.

✦

The window is closing. I said that to myself, in one form or another, for most of my life. In a hostel in Montreal. On a plane from Moscow. On Kwajalein. On a factory floor. On a ship between planets.

I don't say it anymore. Not because I'm done. Because it's not my window now.

It's hers.

The window is open. Keep it that way.
